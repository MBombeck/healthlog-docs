HealthLog shows two closely related numbers: a **readiness** index it computes from your own data, and a **recovery** value that prefers a connected device's native score. This page explains how each is built and — the load-bearing decision — why the device's own recovery wins.

## One recovery, resolved from the strongest source

Several devices report their own overnight recovery or readiness, and HealthLog can also compute a proxy. If those competed on the same surface you would see two different "recovery" numbers for one night. They do not. Recovery resolves to **one canonical value per wake-day** from an ordered ladder:

```
WHOOP  ≻  Oura  ≻  Polar  ≻  computed proxy
```

A device's native recovery is ground truth when you wear it: it is measured against that device's own all-night baseline with sensors HealthLog does not have. The computed proxy is the fallback for a day with no native recovery signal. This ladder is read straight off the single [source-priority](/explanation/source-priority/) list, so adding a future recovery source updates one list and every surface inherits the new rank.

### Why a wake-day key matters

Devices stamp recovery at different instants — a strap stamps the morning it computes the score; the computed proxy stamps the day that ended. Left naïve, the same night's two readings could land on different calendar days and read as two recoveries. The resolver pins each row to the **wake-day** it actually describes, in your timezone, so one night reads as exactly one recovery on the tile, the chart, the doctor-report, the exports, the coach and the companion app.

## The computed readiness proxy

When no device reports recovery, HealthLog computes a 0–100 readiness index — and shows every component that fed it, so the headline is never a black box. It is a **deviation-from-baseline** blend: each component asks "how far is today from *your* normal, measured in your own spread?" rather than comparing you to a population.

The present components are weighted and the weights **renormalise over only the components actually present**, so a missing signal lowers confidence rather than silently scoring zero:

| Component | What it reads |
| --- | --- |
| Resting heart rate | Elevation above your personal baseline |
| HRV | Suppression below your personal baseline |
| Sleep adequacy | The night's [sleep score](/explanation/health-scores/) folded in |
| Respiratory rate | Stability against baseline |
| Mood | Recent mood aggregate |

Below a minimum number of present components no headline is produced — a one-of-five reading is not a readiness score.

### The literature behind RHR and HRV as recovery markers

Using a *rise* in resting heart rate and a *drop* in heart-rate variability as markers of incomplete recovery and accumulated load is well established: Plews et al. (*Sports Med* 2013) on HRV for training-status monitoring, and Buchheit (*Front Physiol* 2014) on monitoring training with heart-rate measures. The personal-baseline framing — comparing today to your own distribution rather than a population — is the [standard-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_score) idea, made outlier-resistant with the [median absolute deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_absolute_deviation) (Hampel 1974; Leys et al. 2013) so a single freak reading does not drag your baseline around.

## A wellness proxy, not a clinical score

The computed readiness is a descriptive wellness framing — not a training-recovery score from a lab and not a diagnosis. When a device reports its own validated recovery, that number is preferred precisely because it is the better measurement. The proxy exists to give a useful signal to people without such a device, and it says so.
