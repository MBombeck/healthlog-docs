Since v1.36.0 one account on an instance can give another account access to its
health record, and since v1.36.1 that access carries a level: read, or read and
add. This page is the reasoning: what that connection is, what it deliberately
is not, why the list of things a second person may add is short and closed, and
which of the instance's existing trust properties it leaves exactly as they
were.

For the steps, see
[Give another account access to your record](/guides/shared-access/).

## It is a grant, never a shared login

The obvious way to let a partner see your blood pressure is to hand them your
password. Every property worth having is lost the moment you do. There is one
identity for two people, so the audit log cannot tell them apart. There is one
second factor, so enrolling a phone means enrolling the other person's. And
taking the access back means changing a password you also use.

A grant keeps both people whole. Each account keeps its own credentials, its own
second factor and its own sessions. The permission is a row that names an owner,
a delegate and a state, and every request the delegate makes is resolved against
that row.

Four properties are the whole of it, and each is decided in one place rather
than at each call site.

**An invitation confers nothing.** A grant that has not been accepted fails the
active check. Being handed access to somebody's health record is not something
the product imposes silently, and the acceptance with its timestamp is what the
row records. That holds twice over at the write level, where accepting also
means agreeing to put entries into somebody else's record under your own name.

**A lapse date is checked live.** The check runs against the clock on the
request that reads the row, not against a sweep that may or may not have run. A
grant a second past its lapse date is already inert.

**Revocation never deletes.** Ending access stamps the row and leaves it in
place. A deleted row cannot answer "who had access, from when to when, and who
ended it", and that question is the reason the table has the shape it does.
Inviting the same person again mints a new row beside the old one.

**The level is fixed at the invitation.** A grant is offered as read or as
write, and it stays what it was offered as. There is no widen call, no upgrade
endpoint, and the absence is the design rather than a gap. Write access needs
two consents: the owner's, because it is their record, and the delegate's,
because they are being asked to write into somebody else's health record.
Acceptance is the single moment those two meet, and it happens once. Raising a
live grant afterwards would carry one consent, and it would not be the
delegate's. The way up is a new invitation at the level meant, accepted again.

State outranks level everywhere. A pending, lapsed or revoked write grant
permits nothing at all, and no level survives an expiry.

## Why write is a list rather than a permission

The reason to share a record is usually that a second person is doing something
about it. Somebody takes the blood pressure, somebody watches the evening dose
go down. Read-only access made them watch that happen and then ask the owner to
type it in, which is the part that fails on a bad evening.

So write exists. What it is not is the mirror image of read. Read is a large
surface because looking at a record cannot damage it. Write is a list of eight
acts, enumerated once, and the list is closed:

- entering a reading, singly or in a batch;
- entering a lab result, which may mint the analyte into the record's own
  catalogue;
- adding a biomarker to that catalogue;
- opening an illness episode;
- adding a value to a metric the owner already tracks;
- recording a side effect against one of the owner's medications;
- adding a medication with its schedule;
- marking a dose taken or skipped.

Every member of that list is a *create*. Nothing on it changes or removes
anything that is already there, and that is the line the feature is built
around. Correcting an entry, deleting one, restoring a deleted one, bulk
actions, and importing a file are the owner's, and they stay the owner's on an
entry the delegate created ten seconds ago with a typo in the dose. A caregiver
who mistypes tells the owner, and the owner fixes it. That is one awkward
sentence in a kitchen, against a delegate quietly rewriting history in somebody
else's health record.

Two absences on that list are decisions rather than oversights.

**Deferring a reminder is not marking a dose.** From a medication card the two
sit side by side and read as the same gesture, and they are not the same act at
all. Recording that a dose went down is a fact about the owner's day. Pushing
the owner's own reminder back is a change to the owner's schedule, made by
somebody who does not have to live with the consequence. It stays with the
owner, and so does changing a dose that is already recorded.

**Allergies and family history are read, not written.** They are entered from a
settings page, and settings is outside what sharing covers. Adding a write path
for them would have meant opening a surface the grant deliberately closes, to
carry two fields that are rarely urgent.

The mechanism under all of this is the same default-deny arm that governs reads.
A route serves a shared record only by declaring which mode it accepts, and a
route that declares nothing refuses outright while a browser is acting on
another account. Nine route modules declare write, and the set is frozen by a
structural test, so a new endpoint cannot quietly join the list. Any unsafe
method on a route that declared itself readable is treated as a write and
refused under a read grant, which means a route cannot widen itself by accident
either.

## Why a delegate's entry is stored as the owner's

An entry a delegate makes lands in the record under the owner's account. It is
not tagged, not flagged, not shown differently, and not separable in an export.
It reads exactly like an entry the owner typed themselves.

That looks like the wrong answer for about a minute. The reasoning is that a
blood-pressure reading is a fact about the owner's body. Who held the cuff is a
fact about an evening. Storing the second one on the row would mean every chart,
every average, every export and every clinician-facing surface has to decide
what to do with a reading that belongs to two people, and the honest answer at
each of those sites is "treat it as the owner's". A column that every reader
resolves the same way is a column that should not exist.

So authorship lives in the audit trail, which is where the question is actually
asked. "Who put this here" has an answer months later without the record itself
having to carry an asterisk, and the owner sees each act as it happens rather
than discovering it in a diff.

The cost is real and worth naming: the audit trail is bounded by
`AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS`, so the entry outlives the name attached to it. On an
instance where households share records, shortening that retention shortens how
long authorship is answerable.

## A refused control is absent, not greyed out

The server decides what a grant admits, and the interface agrees with it rather
than guessing. A control the record will not accept is not rendered.

The alternative was tried and is worse. A delegate opens a page inside somebody
else's record, sees the button, fills the form, and is refused. Nothing leaks,
because the refusal is correct, and the person still walks away believing the
product is broken. A greyed-out button says "this exists and does not work for
you". A control that is not there says "this is not part of what you were
given", which is the truth.

The banner carries what the absence cannot say on its own. It names the record
and the mode in the same breath, at both levels: read-only says the record
cannot be changed, and write says entries can be added but not changed or
deleted. A person who cannot find the edit button reads the banner and knows
why, without a chip on every control explaining itself.

## Why the account is not part of the record

The health record is what sharing covers. Everything wrapped around it stays
with the person who owns the account, and the line is drawn on the server rather
than by hiding menu items.

| Not reachable | Why it would matter |
| --- | --- |
| Password, passkeys, second factor, recovery codes | Silent takeover, surviving any revocation |
| Sessions, trusted devices, sign-out-everywhere | Ending or enumerating the owner's logins |
| API tokens, the MCP connector | A durable credential that outlives the access |
| Connected services (Withings, Fitbit, WHOOP, Google Health, Polar, Oura, Strava, Nightscout) | Re-pointing a sync, or feeding somebody else's wearable into this record |
| Notification channels and push devices | The owner's health alerts redirected to another phone or chat |
| Exports, the health-record archive, clinician share links | A one-click copy of everything, or a new door that outlives the access |
| Insights and the Coach | AI processing of the owner's data, under the owner's own consent |
| Sharing itself | Inviting somebody else, widening the access, or passing it on |
| Locale, theme, dashboard layout | Presentation belongs to the person, not to the record |

Two of those rows are worth a sentence each.

**Sharing is not transferable.** A delegate cannot invite a third person, cannot
raise their own level, and cannot hand their access on. This is the property the
whole feature rests on, and it is why grant management is refused from inside a
shared record rather than merely absent from the navigation.

**The AI row looks arbitrary and is not.** Insights and the Coach send the
record's data to a provider under a consent the record's *owner* gave, for the
owner's own use. Somebody else triggering that would create a consent-shaped act
the owner never made, so those surfaces stay owner-only.

Fifty-two route modules serve a shared record, and nine of those also accept a
write. Every other route in the product refuses by default while a browser is
acting on another account; a route becomes reachable only by declaring which
mode it serves, and both declared sets are frozen by a test. Adding a route
therefore cannot silently widen a delegate's reach, because the default arm
denies.

Two of the refusals are permanent rather than pending. A delegate cannot mint a
clinician share link, because that would be a door surviving their own
revocation. And a delegate cannot reach anything that would widen their own
access.

## What revocation can and cannot do

Ending access takes effect on the delegate's **next request**, not at their next
login. The grant is read from the database on every single request, so there is
no cached verdict to wait out. If their browser is sitting inside the record when
the owner ends it, the same database transaction puts that browser back into its
own account, so they land at home rather than on a wall of refusals.

What revocation cannot do is take back what was already seen. A person who had
read access could have read, remembered, screenshotted or written down anything
in the record. The product can close the door and cannot reach through it.
Nothing in the interface implies otherwise, and neither does this page.

Entries a delegate added stay too, and that is not a gap. They are the owner's
rows, in the owner's record, and the owner has had every one of them named in
the activity view since the moment it was written. Ending access removes the
ability to add more. It is not an undo, and treating it as one would mean a
revocation silently deleting health data the owner may well want to keep. What
the owner keeps is the ordinary ability to correct or delete any of those rows,
which they had all along and the delegate never did.

## What sharing does not change about your instance

A sharing feature is exactly the moment somebody re-reads the trust model, so
this is worth stating plainly.

- **The operator could always read the database.** Anyone with server or
  database access, and anyone with an admin account on this instance, can reach
  health data directly. That was true before sharing existed and is unchanged by
  it. On a household instance the operator is usually one of the people using
  it; on an instance hosting other people, it is worth saying out loud.
- **Encryption at rest is instance-keyed, not per-user.** The encrypted columns
  are protected by the key in `ENCRYPTION_KEYS`, which belongs to the instance.
  Sharing does not weaken that, and it never did protect one account's data from
  the operator of the same instance. See
  [Encryption at rest](/explanation/encryption-at-rest/).
- **Nothing new leaves the instance.** A grant is two accounts on one server
  reading one Postgres. No new egress path exists because of it, and AI
  processing stays under the record owner's consent because the AI surfaces are
  not part of what sharing covers. See
  [Privacy architecture](/explanation/privacy-architecture/).

## Operator obligations

Sharing needs no configuration and does nothing until one account invites
another. Four consequences are yours to know about.

**Invitations disclose whether a username exists.** An invitation to a name
nobody uses is refused with "no account on this instance carries that name".
The alternative is a silent pending row that will never be accepted because the
owner mistyped, which is the worse failure. The caller is already authenticated
and the endpoint allows ten invitations an hour per account, which bounds how
fast the surface could be walked.

**Audit retention bounds what an owner can see.** The view of who opened a
record and what they contributed is built from audit rows, so
`AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS` sets how far back it reaches. The default is 365
days. Shortening it shortens that view, and the page says which number your
instance uses rather than a hardcoded one.

This matters more since write access exists. Authorship of a delegate's entry is
held in the audit trail and nowhere else, so the retention window is the window
in which "who entered this" is answerable at all. The entry itself is permanent.
The name attached to it is not. On an instance where households share records,
that is a retention decision rather than a housekeeping one.

**Marking somebody's dose notifies them by default.** The event is on unless the
owner turns it off, and it rides whichever channels they already enabled. On an
instance with no channel configured it degrades to nothing, quietly, which is
the ordinary behaviour of every other notification and worth knowing before
somebody asks why they were not told.

**Account deletion removes grants and keeps history.** Deleting an account
removes the grants it is party to. Audit rows naming that account as the one who
opened somebody's record stay and render as "a deleted account". Nulling them
would turn "your daughter opened your record" into "you opened your record",
which is worse than an unresolvable id. See
[Data deletion](/account/data-deletion/).

Backups deliberately carry no grants, which matters when you restore. See
[what a backup does not carry](/guides/backups/#what-a-backup-does-not-carry).
