Since v1.36.0 one account on an instance can give another account access to its
health record. Since v1.36.1 that access carries a level, and since v1.37.0
there are three of them, an invitation can name the parts of the record it
opens, and a record can exist for somebody who has no login at all. This page is
the reasoning: what that connection is, what it deliberately is not, why the
levels behave the way they do, and which of the instance's existing trust
properties it leaves exactly as they were.

For the steps, see
[Give another account access to your record](/guides/shared-access/) and
[Records with no login of their own](/guides/managed-profiles/).

## It is a grant, never a shared login

The obvious way to let a partner see your blood pressure is to hand them your
password. Every property worth having is lost the moment you do. There is one
identity for two people, so the audit log cannot tell them apart. There is one
second factor, so enrolling a phone means enrolling the other person's. And
taking the access back means changing a password you also use.

A grant keeps both people whole. Each account keeps its own credentials, its own
second factor and its own sessions. The permission is a row that names an owner,
a delegate and a state, and every request the delegate makes is resolved against
that row.

Four properties are the whole of it, and each is decided in one place rather
than at each call site.

**An invitation confers nothing.** A grant that has not been accepted fails the
active check. Being handed access to somebody's health record is not something
the product imposes silently, and the acceptance with its timestamp is what the
row records. That holds twice over at the write level, where accepting also
means agreeing to put entries into somebody else's record under your own name.

**A lapse date is checked live.** The check runs against the clock on the
request that reads the row, not against a sweep that may or may not have run. A
grant a second past its lapse date is already inert.

**Revocation never deletes.** Ending access stamps the row and leaves it in
place. A deleted row cannot answer "who had access, from when to when, and who
ended it", and that question is the reason the table has the shape it does.
Inviting the same person again mints a new row beside the old one.

**The level and the scope are fixed at the invitation.** A grant is offered as
read, write or manage, over the whole record or over named sections, and it
stays what it was offered as. There is no widen call, no upgrade endpoint, and
the absence is the design rather than a gap. Anything above read needs two
consents: the owner's, because it is their record, and the delegate's, because
they are being asked to write into somebody else's health record. Acceptance is
the single moment those two meet, and it happens once. Raising a live grant
afterwards would carry one consent, and it would not be the delegate's. The way
up is a new invitation at the level meant, accepted again.

State outranks level everywhere. A pending, lapsed or revoked grant permits
nothing at all, whatever it was offered as, and no level survives an expiry.

## Why write is a list rather than a permission

The reason to share a record is usually that a second person is doing something
about it. Somebody takes the blood pressure, somebody watches the evening dose
go down. Read-only access made them watch that happen and then ask the owner to
type it in, which is the part that fails on a bad evening.

So write exists. What it is not is the mirror image of read. Read is a large
surface because looking at a record cannot damage it. Write is a list of acts,
enumerated once, and the list is closed:

- entering a reading, singly or in a batch;
- entering a lab result, which may mint the analyte into the record's own
  catalogue;
- adding a biomarker to that catalogue;
- opening an illness episode;
- recording a side effect against one of the owner's medications;
- adding a medication with its schedule;
- marking a dose taken or skipped, one at a time or by clearing what is due.

Every member of that list is a *create*. Nothing on it changes or removes
anything that is already there, and that is the line the write level is built
around. Correcting an entry, deleting one, restoring a deleted one, bulk
actions, and importing a file are not on it, and they stay off it on an entry
the delegate created ten seconds ago with a typo in the dose. A caregiver who
mistypes tells the owner, and the owner fixes it. That is one awkward sentence
in a kitchen, against a write-level delegate quietly rewriting history in
somebody else's health record.

One entry left that list in v1.37.0 rather than joining it. Logging a value on a
metric the owner tracks themselves was named on the consent screen and had
nothing behind it: the only form that records one sits on a page a shared record
does not open, so nobody could ever have used it. A capability nobody can reach
is worse than an absent one, because the consent screen was telling people they
had granted something. The copy now lists what the level actually admits, and
reading those values was never affected.

Two absences are decisions rather than oversights, and both survive the third
level.

**Deferring a reminder is not marking a dose.** From a medication card the two
sit side by side and read as the same gesture, and they are not the same act at
all. Recording that a dose went down is a fact about the owner's day. Pushing
the owner's own reminder back is a change to the owner's schedule, made by
somebody who does not have to live with the consequence.

**A dose that already carries a decision keeps it.** Re-sending the same
decision is admitted, because a second tap, a phone catching up after no signal
and a retry after a partial sweep all look like that, and somebody standing over
a pill box hits all three. Turning the owner's "taken" into a "skipped" is a
different act and is refused however the request arrives.

The mechanism under all of this is the same default-deny arm that governs reads.
A route serves a shared record only by declaring which mode it accepts and which
section of the record it touches, and a route that declares nothing refuses
outright while a browser is acting on another account. Both declared sets are
frozen by a structural test, so a new endpoint cannot quietly join either list.
Any unsafe method on a route that declared itself readable is treated as a write
and refused under a read grant, which means a route cannot widen itself by
accident either.

## Why there is a third level, and where it stops

Write is a caregiver level and it has a shape: somebody who is present, doing
things, and reporting them. There is a second relationship it does not describe.
Somebody may be keeping the record rather than contributing to it, and keeping
it means fixing the row that is wrong.

Manage is that. It adds changing and removing entries, including ones the owner
wrote, recording the record's own health background, and reading the insights
generated from the record.

Three properties make it something other than "full access".

**The boundary is record content against account configuration.** Manage never
reaches the login, the second factor, provider connections, API tokens,
notification routing, module and threshold configuration, or who else has
access. That is the same line the other two levels stop at, and it is the line
that makes a grant a grant instead of a shared account. The one thing that moved
across it in v1.37.0 is the health background: allergies and family history live
under Settings in the interface, but they are facts about a body rather than
configuration of an account, so a manager may record them.

**Offering it can only happen from a browser session,** and when the owner has
a second factor enrolled, the offer demands a fresh step-up through that
session first. An account that never enrolled a second factor is not blocked
from offering manage; it simply has no factor to be asked for. (Creating a
managed profile is the surface with the unconditional fresh-MFA gate.)
Accepting it needs
nothing special, because the consent that matters at that end is the delegate
agreeing to act under their own name.

**Everything a manager does is named.** The activity view does not say "made a
change". It says which entry, and with which verb: changed a reading, removed an
illness entry, recorded an illness day. A level that can rewrite the record is
only tolerable if the owner can read what was rewritten.

Manage always covers the entire record. That is not a simplification, it is the
same reasoning the next section is about.

## Why a scope can name sections but a manage grant cannot

An invitation can open part of a record instead of all of it. Eight sections,
each one a thing a person recognises as one thing, and everything not picked
stays closed. A section held back is refused exactly the way a record nobody
shared is, so the shape of what was held back is not visible from outside.

The section names are a sharing vocabulary and not the module vocabulary the
instance already has, deliberately and permanently. The two answer different
questions. "Did the owner turn this part of the product on" and "did the owner
open this part of their record to that person" are unrelated, and the day they
share a spelling is the day somebody makes one imply the other. The implication
would run in the dangerous direction: a module the owner enabled is not a
section the owner shared.

Two limits are published on the consent screen rather than left for somebody to
discover.

**A section carries whatever was written in it.** Notes and names inside one
section can mention any other part of a person's health, so sharing a section
shares everything written in it. The vault is the sharpest case, because a
document can contain anything at all. Saying this on the screen where the choice
is made is the honest version; the alternative is a scope that reads tighter
than it is.

**Whole-record figures need the whole record.** The dashboard overview, the
health score and the daily digest appear only when the entire record is shared,
because a figure computed from part of a record still reads as a figure about
the person, and nothing on the surface would say which parts it left out.

That second point is also why a manage grant is whole-record by construction. A
level that can rewrite entries, over a slice of a record whose notes can be
about any other slice, is a boundary that cannot be honestly drawn. An
invitation naming both a scope and manage is refused rather than quietly
narrowed to one of them.

A stored scope never grows by release either. A grant written when the list held
eight sections opens those of the eight it named, and nothing added later, since
the scope is stored as the keys the owner ticked and a key that did not exist
then cannot be in it. A new section is therefore a consent question rather than
a schema question. Every grant written before v1.37.0 carries no scope at all,
which means the entire record, which is what those owners consented to.

## Why a record can exist without a login

A grant runs between two accounts, and it covers exactly half of looking after
somebody. The other half is the person who has no account to begin with: a
child, or somebody in your care. Giving them a login they will never use, so
that somebody else can hold its password, would recreate the shared-login
problem this feature exists to avoid.

So a managed profile is a record without credentials. No password, no passkey,
no second factor, and no path to gaining one. The people who look after it hold
manage grants with the profile as the grantor, which means everything already
true of a grant is true here: live revocation, an audit trail under the actor's
own name, and no reach into account configuration.

Three consequences follow from a record with nobody behind it.

**Creating one is a step-up act.** It mints a real record on the instance, and
the creator becomes its first guardian in the same transaction, so there is no
instant where the record exists and nobody is looking after it.

**Its reminders belong to its guardians.** A record with no login has no phone
and no chat. Medication and measurement reminders, safety-floor alerts and
low-stock alerts go to the guardians instead, each in their own language and on
the channels they already chose, and the message names whose record it is about.
It carries no action buttons: a lock screen is a poor place to decide something
about another person's medication, so opening the app takes you through the
ordinary switch into the record.

**It can never be left empty.** The last guardian may not hand the record back
and may not be removed, and a pending invitation does not satisfy the floor
because nothing about it is accepted yet. The way out is to add somebody else or
to delete the profile. Deleting is a real ending and it is offered plainly,
because "you may end this record" and "you may walk away from it" are different
permissions and only the first one is safe.

The floor holds on every route that could breach it, including a guardian
deleting their own account and a data wipe, rather than only on the button that
looks like it would.

## Why a delegate's entry is stored as the owner's

An entry a delegate makes lands in the record under the owner's account. It is
not tagged, not flagged, not shown differently, and not separable in an export.
It reads exactly like an entry the owner typed themselves.

That looks like the wrong answer for about a minute. The reasoning is that a
blood-pressure reading is a fact about the owner's body. Who held the cuff is a
fact about an evening. Storing the second one on the row would mean every chart,
every average, every export and every clinician-facing surface has to decide
what to do with a reading that belongs to two people, and the honest answer at
each of those sites is "treat it as the owner's". A column that every reader
resolves the same way is a column that should not exist.

So authorship lives in the audit trail, which is where the question is actually
asked. "Who put this here" has an answer months later without the record itself
having to carry an asterisk, and the owner sees each act as it happens rather
than discovering it in a diff.

The cost is real and worth naming: the audit trail is bounded by
`AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS`, so the entry outlives the name attached to it. On an
instance where households share records, shortening that retention shortens how
long authorship is answerable.

## A refused control is absent, not greyed out

The server decides what a grant admits, and the interface agrees with it rather
than guessing. A control the record will not accept is not rendered.

The alternative was tried and is worse. A delegate opens a page inside somebody
else's record, sees the button, fills the form, and is refused. Nothing leaks,
because the refusal is correct, and the person still walks away believing the
product is broken. A greyed-out button says "this exists and does not work for
you". A control that is not there says "this is not part of what you were
given", which is the truth.

The banner carries what the absence cannot say on its own. It names the record
and the mode in the same breath: read-only says the record cannot be changed,
and write says entries can be added but not changed or deleted. A person who
cannot find the edit button reads the banner and knows why, without a chip on
every control explaining itself.

There is a moment before the answer arrives, and it is handled rather than
tolerated. A control tapped while the app still does not know whose record is on
screen used to strand somebody in a form the server was going to refuse. The
capture sheet and the dashboard's quick-entry sheets now withdraw when the
answer lands, which is what the measurements page and the medication wizard
already did. Standing on a form that has become invalid is a worse outcome than
closing it under somebody's hands.

## Why the account is not part of the record

The health record is what sharing covers. Everything wrapped around it stays
with the person who owns the account, and the line is drawn on the server rather
than by hiding menu items.

| Not reachable | Why it would matter |
| --- | --- |
| Password, passkeys, second factor, recovery codes | Silent takeover, surviving any revocation |
| Sessions, trusted devices, sign-out-everywhere | Ending or enumerating the owner's logins |
| API tokens, the MCP connector | A durable credential that outlives the access |
| Connected services (Withings, Fitbit, WHOOP, Google Health, Polar, Oura, Strava, Nightscout) | Re-pointing a sync, or feeding somebody else's wearable into this record |
| Notification channels and push devices | The owner's health alerts redirected to another phone or chat |
| Exports, the health-record archive, clinician share links | A one-click copy of everything, or a new door that outlives the access |
| Generating an insight, and the Coach | AI processing of the owner's data, under the owner's own consent |
| Module switches, thresholds and notification routing | Configuration of the account, not content of the record |
| Sharing itself | Inviting somebody else, widening the access, or passing it on |
| Locale, theme, dashboard layout | Presentation belongs to the person, not to the record |

Three of those rows are worth a sentence each.

**Sharing is not transferable.** A delegate cannot invite a third person, cannot
raise their own level, and cannot hand their access on. This is the property the
whole feature rests on, and it is why grant management is refused from inside a
shared record rather than merely absent from the navigation.

**The AI row looks arbitrary and is not.** Generating an insight and talking to
the Coach send the record's data to a provider under a consent the record's
*owner* gave, for the owner's own use. Somebody else triggering that would
create a consent-shaped act the owner never made, so those surfaces stay
owner-only. Reading an insight that already exists is a different act, and a
manage-level delegate may do it: nothing leaves the instance, and the reason
somebody is keeping a record is often exactly to read what it concluded.

**The document vault is read, at every level.** Reading a clinical letter is
health data belonging to the record and much of the reason to hand somebody the
record at all. Uploading, retyping, discarding, linking a document to an illness
episode and every AI action stay with the owner, so the vault behaves the same
whether the grant is read, write or manage.

Every route in the product refuses by default while a browser is acting on
another account. A route becomes reachable only by declaring which mode it
serves and which section of the record it touches, and every declared set is
frozen by a test. Adding a route therefore cannot silently widen a delegate's
reach, because the default arm denies.

That default has one honest limit, and it is worth stating for anyone reading
the API contract. The published OpenAPI document does not describe every route
that can serve a shared record; a number of them predate the contract and have
never been published. The refusal is identical on all of them, and a guard
prevents an unpublished one being published without it, but "the contract is
complete and consistent" is not the same claim as "every delegable route is
described".

Two of the refusals are permanent rather than pending. A delegate cannot mint a
clinician share link, because that would be a door surviving their own
revocation. And a delegate cannot reach anything that would widen their own
access.

## What revocation can and cannot do

Ending access takes effect on the delegate's **next request**, not at their next
login. The grant is read from the database on every single request, so there is
no cached verdict to wait out. If their browser is sitting inside the record when
the owner ends it, the same database transaction puts that browser back into its
own account, so they land at home rather than on a wall of refusals.

What revocation cannot do is take back what was already seen. A person who had
read access could have read, remembered, screenshotted or written down anything
in the record. The product can close the door and cannot reach through it.
Nothing in the interface implies otherwise, and neither does this page.

Entries a delegate added stay too, and that is not a gap. They are the owner's
rows, in the owner's record, and the owner has had every one of them named in
the activity view since the moment it was written. Ending access removes the
ability to act further. It is not an undo, and treating it as one would mean a
revocation silently deleting health data the owner may well want to keep. What
the owner keeps is the ordinary ability to correct or delete any of those rows,
which they had all along.

## Why every refusal reads the same

A delegate who is refused gets one status and one body, whatever the reason.
A record nobody shared with them, an account that does not exist at all, a
section the grant does not open, a write on a read grant: identical bytes.

That is the point rather than a missing feature. A refusal that told the caller
which of those it was would be a way for anybody with a login on the instance to
find out which accounts exist and what they have. The reason does not vanish;
it goes where it belongs, which is the record owner's activity feed and the
operator's audit trail.

The consequence for anyone building against the API is short: on that refusal,
stop using the selector, re-read the account payload, and render what it now
says. There is nothing to be learned by telling the cases apart, because they
were deliberately made indistinguishable.

## Why a request names the record it thinks it is in

Switching records moves the browser session rather than the tab you clicked in.
That is the right behaviour, because a person is in one record at a time and
they should not have to switch each window separately. It also left a hole: a
second tab, open on the record you just left, went on reading and writing there
until somebody reloaded it.

So a browser request carries the record the tab believes it is in, and a request
naming a record the session has left is refused with an answer the tab recovers
from by re-reading who it is. The recovery is not a failure and is never
presented as one.

Three properties keep that from costing anything.

A session that has never opened somebody else's record carries nothing and is
unaffected, which is what keeps an open tab working across a deploy. Once a
session has entered a shared record it carries the assertion permanently, so
there is no window where the check can be skipped by going home first. And the
refusal body deliberately carries no context to re-adopt: a refusal is not a
third place to learn from, so the tab goes back to the one surface that answers
that question.

None of this applies to the phone app or to any other API client authenticating
with a token. Those name the record on each request already and never held a
selector that could go stale, so they see none of it.

## What sharing does not change about your instance

A sharing feature is exactly the moment somebody re-reads the trust model, so
this is worth stating plainly.

- **The operator could always read the database.** Anyone with server or
  database access, and anyone with an admin account on this instance, can reach
  health data directly. That was true before sharing existed and is unchanged by
  it. On a household instance the operator is usually one of the people using
  it; on an instance hosting other people, it is worth saying out loud.
- **Encryption at rest is instance-keyed, not per-user.** The encrypted columns
  are protected by the key in `ENCRYPTION_KEYS`, which belongs to the instance.
  Sharing does not weaken that, and it never did protect one account's data from
  the operator of the same instance. See
  [Encryption at rest](/explanation/encryption-at-rest/).
- **Nothing new leaves the instance.** A grant is two accounts on one server
  reading one Postgres. No new egress path exists because of it, and AI
  processing stays under the record owner's consent because the AI surfaces are
  not part of what sharing covers. See
  [Privacy architecture](/explanation/privacy-architecture/).

## Operator obligations

Sharing needs no configuration and does nothing until one account invites
another. Five consequences are yours to know about.

**Invitations disclose whether a username exists.** An invitation to a name
nobody uses is refused with "no account on this instance carries that name".
The alternative is a silent pending row that will never be accepted because the
owner mistyped, which is the worse failure. The caller is already authenticated
and the endpoint allows ten invitations an hour per account, which bounds how
fast the surface could be walked.

**Audit retention bounds what an owner can see.** The view of who opened a
record and what they did in it is built from audit rows, so
`AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS` sets how far back it reaches. The default is 365 days
and the floor is 7: a smaller value is ignored rather than obeyed, so a window
entered in seconds by mistake cannot empty a fresh table. Shortening it shortens
that view, and the interface says which number your instance uses rather than a
hardcoded one. It says it in two places, the caption under the activity list and
the dialog that ends somebody's access, and both take the value the server
resolved from your variable. See
[Audit-trail retention](/reference/environment-variables/#audit-trail-retention).

This matters more with every level above read. Authorship of a delegate's entry
is held in the audit trail and nowhere else, so the retention window is the
window in which "who entered this" is answerable at all. The entry itself is
permanent. The name attached to it is not. On an instance where households share
records, that is a retention decision rather than a housekeeping one.

The list also states when it is not showing everything. A busy record can
produce more activity than one read returns, and a list that stopped at its own
ceiling in silence would let the oldest visible line read as the beginning.

**A managed profile is a full record with no login.** It counts against storage,
produces rollups and reminders like any other record, is covered by the same
backups and the same instance key, and never appears in a sign-in log because it
has no sign-in. Its reminders fan out to its guardians, so one dose-due event on
a profile with three guardians becomes three notifications on three sets of
channels. Creating one requires a second factor on the creating account, which
is structural rather than a policy setting.

**Marking somebody's dose notifies them by default.** The event is on unless the
owner turns it off, and it rides whichever channels they already enabled. On an
instance with no channel configured it degrades to nothing, quietly, which is
the ordinary behaviour of every other notification and worth knowing before
somebody asks why they were not told.

**Account deletion removes grants and keeps history.** Deleting an account
removes the grants it is party to. Audit rows naming that account as the one who
opened somebody's record stay and render as "a deleted account". Nulling them
would turn "your daughter opened your record" into "you opened your record",
which is worse than an unresolvable id. See
[Data deletion](/account/data-deletion/).

Backups deliberately carry no grants, which matters when you restore. See
[what a backup does not carry](/guides/backups/#what-a-backup-does-not-carry).
