A self-hosted server cannot push to the App Store app on its own, so the
[push relay](/guides/push-relay/) carries notifications to Apple for it. This
page explains why the relay cannot read what it carries.

## What is protected and how

**Keys made on the phone.** When you turn the relay on, the iPhone creates its
keys with Apple CryptoKit: an X25519 device key for opening notifications and
an Ed25519 binding key. The private halves stay in the iPhone keychain,
reachable only by the app and its notification extension. Only the public keys
go to your server.

**Sealed on your server.** Your server seals every notification for that one
device with HPKE (RFC 9180), using X25519, HKDF-SHA256 and ChaCha20-Poly1305.
It uses auth mode, which means the server's own sender key goes into the seal.
The phone pins that key, so an envelope opens only if it came from your
server. Each envelope has a fixed size per class and its own expiry.

**A binding only the phone can make.** The relay links a device to a server
only with a statement signed by the phone's binding key. Another server that
once saw the device's push token cannot take it over, and the phone can
revoke the binding at the relay directly, without asking your server.

**Signed answers.** Every answer from the relay is signed with the relay's
Ed25519 key, which your server and the app pin. A forged answer is treated
as a network error.

## What the relay and Apple can still see

They see that your server sent a device something, when, of which class, and
the fixed size of the envelope. They see the device's push token, and Apple
can link that token to an Apple account. They never see medication names,
doses, readings or any other content.

Timing is the honest limit. If you record a dose on one device and another
device of yours gets a confirmation, the moment of that push can hint at when
you take your medication. That is true of every iOS push service, and it is
why reminders are planned on the iPhone and not sent through the relay.

## If something goes wrong

**The relay is down.** Reminders the app has scheduled on the iPhone keep
working. Confirmations between devices and the silent sync pause, and
the app says so.

**The relay is compromised.** An attacker still cannot open an envelope,
because the relay never holds a key that does. They could put a notification
on a device whose token they have seen, but the app acts only on envelopes
sealed with a sender key it pinned. The response is to revoke the Apple push
key and switch the relay to its next identity key, which your server and the
app already pin. You can also revoke the device's binding from the app at any
time, even while your server is unreachable.
