The menstrual cycle is an accessible window on reproductive and overall health — regular enough to recognise a pattern, variable enough that the pattern is personal. This page explains the four phases, how to read length and regularity, and where the honest limits of calendar prediction lie.

## What it is

The cycle is a recurring hormonal sequence across four phases.[^nichd2024][^statpearls2023]

{/* IMAGE: a four-phase cycle diagram — a horizontal timeline showing menstrual, follicular, ovulatory and luteal phases with the estrogen peak before ovulation and the progesterone rise (and small basal-body-temperature step-up) across the luteal phase. Alt text: "Diagram of the four menstrual-cycle phases showing the estrogen peak before ovulation and the progesterone-driven basal temperature rise in the luteal phase." */}

- **Menstrual** — estrogen and progesterone are low and the uterine lining sheds.
- **Follicular** — FSH recruits follicles, and rising estrogen rebuilds the lining.
- **Ovulatory** — estrogen peaks, triggering the LH surge; ovulation follows roughly 36–44 hours after the surge begins.
- **Luteal** — the corpus luteum produces progesterone, which matures the lining and slightly raises basal body temperature.

## Why it matters

Cycle length and regularity are an accessible signal of reproductive and general health — the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists frames the cycle as a "vital sign."[^acog2015] The cycle also interacts with [body temperature](/knowledge/temperature/), mood, sleep and heart-rate variability, so tracking it adds context to those metrics rather than standing alone.

## How to read your cycle

Two reputable bodies publish slightly different "typical" adult cycle-length ranges, and both are worth citing because they answer different questions — one patient-facing, one a clinical screening frame.

| Measure | Typical range | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult cycle length (patient-facing) | 21–35 days | NICHD/NIH, 2024 |
| Adult cycle length (clinical "vital sign") | 24–38 days | ACOG, 2015 |
| Adolescent cycle | wider and more variable than adult | ACOG, 2015 |
| Bleeding duration | about 5 days (up to roughly 7) | ACOG, 2015 |
| Luteal phase | about 12–14 days (12–14 typical; 10–16 with within-person variability) | StatPearls, 2023 |

The luteal phase is the relatively constant anchor — most of the variation in total cycle length comes from the follicular phase. Cycle-to-cycle variation of about a week is common: roughly 46% of women aged 18–40 see it.[^nichd2024] Because of that, ovulation is best estimated by counting *back* from your next expected period, never fixed at "day 14."

### The fertile window, and why prediction is approximate

The fertile window is the six days ending on the day of ovulation — the five days before ovulation plus ovulation day itself.[^wilcox2000] Ovulation timing is highly variable even in people with regular cycles: in only about 30% of women does the fertile window fall entirely within the textbook days 10–17.[^wilcox2000] Calendar estimates need at least three recorded cycles — ideally six to twelve — and stay approximate even then.

Confirmation signals help. An LH surge (urine ovulation test) predicts ovulation about 12–36 hours ahead; a basal-temperature rise and a cervical-mucus peak confirm it only retrospectively. The symptothermal method, which double-checks mucus against basal temperature, is the recognised fertility-awareness gold standard.[^statpearls2023]

<Aside type="caution">
Algorithmic cycle predictions — including any wearable or app — are **not a contraceptive method**. Population-default predictions are unreliable, and perimenopause makes cycles variable again. Anyone using fertility awareness to prevent or plan pregnancy should use a validated, properly instructed method, not a single predicted date.
</Aside>

## What changes it

Cycle length and regularity shift with age (adolescence and perimenopause are both more variable), significant changes in body weight or training load, stress, illness, thyroid and other endocrine conditions, hormonal contraception, and pregnancy. A single unusual cycle is rarely meaningful on its own; a *sustained change from your own established pattern* is the more informative signal — which is exactly why tracking your personal baseline matters more than comparing yourself to a population range.

## When to act

Cycle thresholds are education signals that route you to a clinician — they are never something to self-interpret or self-diagnose.

<Aside type="caution">
Raise it with a clinician when cycles consistently fall outside the typical range — recurring intervals shorter than roughly 21 days or longer than about 45 days — or when there is a marked, sustained change from your own established pattern.[^acog2015]

Absent menses also warrants evaluation: any single gap of 90 days or more (about three months) in someone who previously menstruated, or a first period that has not arrived by age 15.[^acog2015]

Bleeding counts as heavy or prolonged when it lasts more than 7 days, or soaks through a pad or tampon more often than every one to two hours.[^acog2015]

Bleeding between periods or after intercourse is worth reporting. Seek urgent care for bleeding accompanied by light-headedness, dizziness, a racing pulse or chest pain.[^acog2015]
</Aside>
