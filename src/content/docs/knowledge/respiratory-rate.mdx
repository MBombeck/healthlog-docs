Respiratory rate is the number of complete breaths you take per minute at rest. Clinically it is counted over 30–60 seconds; wearables estimate it overnight from chest-wall motion, subtle modulation of the optical pulse signal, or the coupling between heart and breath. It is one of the most sensitive and earliest signs of clinical deterioration — and, historically, one of the most poorly recorded vital signs.

## Why it matters

Breathing rate rises (tachypnoea) with fever, infection, sepsis, metabolic acidosis, pain, anxiety, an asthma or COPD exacerbation, pulmonary embolism and heart failure. It falls (bradypnoea) with central-nervous-system or respiratory depression from opioids and sedatives, severe hypothyroidism, or rising intracranial pressure.[^statpearls] An elevated *overnight baseline* is an early, non-specific signal that something is brewing — which is what makes the trend, rather than any single count, the useful part.

## How to read your numbers

| Category | Range | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Normal resting adult | 12–20 breaths/min | American Lung Association; StatPearls/NIH, 2023 [^ala] [^statpearls] |
| Tachypnoea | >20/min | StatPearls/NIH, 2023 [^statpearls] |
| Sepsis-screening threshold (qSOFA) | ≥22/min | Sepsis-3, *JAMA*, 2016 [^sepsis3] |
| Common concern cutoff | ≥25/min | clinical convention |
| Highest-risk scoring (NEWS2) | ≤8 or ≥25/min | RCP, 2017 [^news2] |
| Bradypnoea | &lt;12/min | StatPearls/NIH, 2023 [^statpearls] |

Children breathe faster: newborns around 30–60/min, infants 24–40, toddlers 20–30, school-age 18–25, and adolescents approaching the adult 12–20. Trained endurance athletes sit at the low end of the adult band.

## What changes it

Respiratory rate is highly state-dependent — talking, movement, emotion, recent activity, even the awareness of being measured, all shift it. It is only meaningful truly at rest or asleep, which is why overnight wearable estimates are more useful than a daytime spot reading. That estimate is an average over the night, not a clinical count, and it degrades with motion or arrhythmia.

The most informative way to read it is **against your own baseline**. A trend of roughly +3 breaths/min above your nightly baseline can flag deterioration and often beats any absolute number — and it is infection-generic, not tied to one illness.[^natarajan] In a study of symptomatic individuals, about a third had at least one night with respiratory rate ≥3/min above baseline within a 7-day onset window.[^miller]

<Aside type="note" title="The delta is the signal">
Because resting rates vary between people, HealthLog leads with deviation from your personal overnight baseline and uses the 12–20/min population band only as a secondary sanity check. A rate that is normal for the population can still be a real rise for you.
</Aside>

## When to act

<Aside type="caution" title="Breathing-rate warning signs">
A breathing rate above 25/min with breathlessness, confusion or a low [SpO₂](/knowledge/spo2/), or any rate at the extreme ends of scoring tools (≤8 or ≥25/min), needs prompt attention.[^news2] Tachypnoea is sensitive but non-specific, so weigh it with how you feel and your other signs. Slow breathing (bradypnoea) — especially opioid-related respiratory depression — is an emergency: if someone is breathing very slowly, is hard to rouse, or has bluish lips, call emergency services. General guidance, not medical advice; individual variation is wide.
</Aside>

## How it connects

Respiratory rate is one of the earliest movers in the early-illness picture. A sustained overnight rise tends to travel with a rising resting heart rate, a falling HRV, and a small rise in skin or body [temperature](/knowledge/temperature/) — the concordance of two or three of these raises confidence far more than any one alone.[^statpearls] Read alongside a falling [SpO₂](/knowledge/spo2/), it points more specifically at the lungs.
