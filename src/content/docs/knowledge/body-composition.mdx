Body composition splits your weight into its parts — how much is fat, how much is lean tissue, where the fat sits, how much is water and bone. It answers the question a scale weight cannot: two people at the same BMI can carry very different amounts of fat, and the difference matters more than the shared number.

## What the numbers measure

- **Body-fat percentage** — fat mass as a fraction of total mass.
- **Visceral fat** — fat packed around the abdominal organs, the component most tightly linked to metabolic disease.
- **Lean / muscle / fat-free mass** — everything that is not fat.
- **Total body water and bone mass** — supporting estimates, best read as stability over time.

These are measured by DXA (the practical reference), bioelectrical impedance (consumer scales and wearables), skinfolds, or hydrostatic and air-displacement methods.

<Aside type="note" title="Consumer impedance is an estimate">
Bioelectrical-impedance scales can err by several percentage points and drift with hydration, recent meals, recent exercise and electrode placement. DXA is the reference method. Read a smart scale for its trend on consistent conditions, not for a single decimal.
</Aside>

## Why it matters

Composition captures the adiposity that BMI misses, distinguishing a muscular frame from a fatty one at the same weight. Visceral fat is the part most strongly tied to type-2 diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular disease.[^vat] At the other end, very low body fat — especially in women — impairs hormonal and reproductive function, so lower is not simply better.

## How to read your numbers

### Body-fat percentage

<Aside type="caution" title="These bands are fitness conventions, not a health standard">
The body-fat categories below are fitness-industry conventions aligned with ACE and ACSM. They are **not** a WHO or CDC standard the way the BMI cut-offs are. Treat them as orientation, and remember that a percentage only means anything once fat mass is divided by body weight.
</Aside>

| Category | Men | Women | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essential | 2–5% | 10–13% | ACE; ACSM[^ace][^acsm] |
| Athletes | 6–13% | 14–20% | ACE; ACSM[^ace][^acsm] |
| Fitness | 14–17% | 21–24% | ACE; ACSM[^ace][^acsm] |
| Acceptable / average | 18–24% | 25–31% | ACE; ACSM[^ace][^acsm] |
| Obesity | ≥25% | ≥32% | ACE; ACSM[^ace][^acsm] |

Women carry more essential fat than men, which is why the bands are sex-specific. Sustained body fat below roughly 14% in women or 6% in men is hazardous, and below about 17% can disrupt menstruation.[^ace] As with BMI, body-fat percentage at a given weight rises with age.

### Visceral fat

There are two separate literatures here, and conflating them produces nonsense numbers.

- **Imaging (CT/MRI/DXA), measured in cm²** — a visceral-fat area above roughly 100 cm² is a widely cited increased-risk threshold, with ~160 cm² often cited as high-risk. The cut-off is ancestry-dependent (lower in Asian populations) and there is no single universal value.[^vat]
- **Consumer-scale "visceral fat rating", a unitless index** — typically on a ~1–59 scale, with roughly 1–12 treated as healthy and 13 and above as elevated by common convention. This rating is **not** a cm² value and does not convert to one.

<Aside type="note" title="Keep the rating and the area apart">
A scale's visceral-fat rating and an imaging cm² figure measure related things on incomparable scales. Track the rating against its own past values; do not read it as square centimetres.
</Aside>

### Lean, muscle and fat-free mass

There is no clean universal "normal kilograms" band — lean mass is height-, sex- and frame-dependent. The honest framing is trend over time against your own baseline, not a population cut-off. Clinical sarcopenia cut-points exist (the appendicular skeletal-muscle mass index in the EWGSOP2 consensus), but they are protocol-specific and DXA/BIA-dependent, which makes them a "discuss with a clinician" pointer rather than a consumer tile.[^ewgsop2]

### Total body water and bone mass

As rough adult orientation, total body water runs about 50–65% of body weight in men and 45–60% in women, lower as fat mass rises — and these are estimates, not measurements.[^tbw] Bone mass from a consumer scale is a low-precision figure best read for stability; DXA is the real tool for bone density, and you should not read osteoporosis into a scale number.

## What changes your numbers

Composition shifts slowly where it counts and quickly where it does not. Fat and lean mass change over weeks of diet and training; the day-to-day wobble a scale reports is mostly hydration and measurement variance. Body-fat bands shift upward with age at any given weight. Because impedance readings move with how recently you ate, drank or trained, take them under consistent conditions — same time of day, similar hydration — or the trend dissolves into noise.

The 2025 *Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology* Commission elevates body-fat and anthropometric measures to confirm clinical and preclinical obesity alongside BMI — formal recognition that composition adds what a single weight cannot.[^lancet2025]

## When to act

<Aside type="caution" title="Read trends, escalate symptoms — not a scale decimal">
No single body-composition reading from a consumer device warrants an alarm; the measurement error is too large. The signals worth a clinician's attention are persistent ones with context — a sustained drop in muscle mass with weakness or falls in an older adult, or very low body fat with loss of menstruation. Sarcopenia and bone-density concerns are clinical assessments (DXA and protocol-specific cut-points), not figures to self-diagnose from a scale.[^ewgsop2]
</Aside>

## How this connects to your other metrics

Body composition is the companion to scale weight: where [weight and BMI](/knowledge/weight-bmi/) give you a fast, reliable trend that cannot see inside, composition shows what the weight is made of. Visceral fat is the thread linking this page to metabolic health — it tracks with insulin resistance and raised glucose, so it is worth reading alongside [blood glucose and HbA1c](/knowledge/glucose-hba1c/). Together with waist measures, composition is how HealthLog follows the 2025 Commission's guidance to confirm adiposity beyond BMI alone.

HealthLog reads these composition metrics as trends against reference bands. They do not enter the composite score directly: its adiposity pillar is driven by the waist-to-height ratio, not by body-fat percentage, visceral rating, lean mass or bone mass.
