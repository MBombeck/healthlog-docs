Pain is whatever the person experiencing it says it is. Unlike a heart rate or a lab value, it has no external sensor — it is inherently subjective and self-reported, and the standard rating scales exist to turn that private experience into a number you can track. The most widely used are the 0–10 Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), where 0 is no pain and the top of the scale is the worst pain imaginable.[^hawker]

## Why it matters

A single pain score is a snapshot of a moving, personal experience; its real value emerges over time. Recording pain consistently lets you see whether a problem is settling or worsening, whether a treatment is helping, and how pain travels with sleep, mood, activity and the rhythm of a condition. That trend — your own pattern and your response to therapy — is far more informative than any one rating, because what counts as "a 4" differs from person to person and even from day to day.[^hawker]

## How to read your numbers

The bands below are a common convention for grouping severity on the 0–10 scale. Treat them as orientation, not a target: the aim is rarely a fixed number but a meaningful, sustained reduction from *your own* baseline.

| Band | NRS / VAS score (0–10) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| No pain | 0 | Hawker et al., 2011 [^hawker] |
| Mild | 1–3 | Boonstra et al., 2014 [^boonstra] |
| Moderate | 4–6 | Boonstra et al., 2014 [^boonstra] |
| Severe | 7–10 | Boonstra et al., 2014 [^boonstra] |

<Aside type="note" title="Track your own trajectory, not the band">
Because pain is self-reported and the cut-points between mild, moderate and severe vary between people, the band a score falls into matters less than how that score moves. A change of roughly 2 points on the 0–10 scale — or about a 30% reduction — is a useful marker of a clinically meaningful improvement to most people, which is why recording pain at consistent times, and noting what you were doing, tells you more than a single rating ever can.[^farrar]
</Aside>

## What changes it

The same underlying problem can be rated very differently depending on context. Sleep loss, low mood, stress and fatigue tend to amplify perceived pain; distraction, rest, treatment and a good night's sleep tend to lower it. The scale used, the wording of the question, the time of day and recent activity all shift a reading, so keep the method consistent — the same scale, framed the same way — when you track over time. Pain that worsens with movement, that wakes you at night, or that follows a clear daily or monthly rhythm is itself useful information to record and bring to a clinician.

## When to act

<Aside type="caution" title="Some pain needs prompt care regardless of the score">
A pain *score* is for tracking, not triage. Pain that is new and severe, sudden and worst-ever, or accompanied by red-flag features needs prompt medical attention rather than logging — for example chest pain or pressure, especially with breathlessness, sweating or pain spreading to the arm or jaw; the sudden "worst headache of your life"; pain with weakness, numbness, trouble speaking or a facial droop; severe abdominal pain; or pain with a high fever, confusion or a stiff neck. When in doubt about severe or sudden pain, call emergency services. General guidance, not medical advice; individual variation is wide.
</Aside>

## How this connects

Pain rarely moves alone. It both worsens and is worsened by poor [sleep](/knowledge/sleep/), and the two are worth reading together — a flare often shows up as restless nights before anything else. It also tracks closely with [mood](/knowledge/mood/): persistent pain lowers mood, and low mood lowers the pain threshold, so logging both side by side helps disentangle which is leading.
