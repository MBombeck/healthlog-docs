Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the most durable predictors of long-term health, and it is one of the few you can move. This page explains what VO₂max measures, how weekly activity and daily steps map onto the evidence, and how to read your own numbers against standards that adjust for age and sex rather than a single fixed target.

## What VO₂max is

VO₂max is the maximal rate at which your body can take up and use oxygen during incremental exercise, expressed in millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body mass per minute (mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹). It is the best single laboratory index of aerobic fitness. A device on your wrist does not measure it — it produces a *model estimate* from the relationship between your heart rate and movement, so treat a wearable VO₂max as a trend line, not a calibrated value.

Activity is the behaviour underneath the number: how many minutes you spend at each intensity each week, how many muscle-strengthening sessions you complete, and how many steps you take.

## Why it matters

Cardiorespiratory fitness is an independent, graded predictor of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Each one-MET increment (roughly 3.5 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ of additional fitness) is associated with about a 10–25% lower mortality risk.[^aha2016] Accumulated aerobic minutes combined with resistance training carry the strongest dose-response evidence in all of preventive medicine — for reduced cardiovascular disease, type-2 diabetes, several cancers, depression and death from any cause.[^who2020][^hhs2018]

## How to read your VO₂max

VO₂max is interpreted as a **percentile within your own age and sex band**, never against a fixed number. Men test roughly 15–20% higher than women at the same age, and fitness declines with age, so a value that is excellent at 60 would be below average at 25. The table below shows the 50th-percentile (median) value by decade from the FRIEND Registry, the reference dataset underpinning the widely used fitness categories.[^friend2015]

| Age band | Men (mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) | Women (mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20–29 | 48.0 | 37.6 |
| 30–39 | 42.4 | 30.2 |
| 40–49 | 37.8 | 26.7 |
| 50–59 | 32.6 | 23.4 |
| 60–69 | 28.2 | 20.0 |
| 70–79 | 24.4 | 18.3 |

Within each age and sex band, fitness is graded by percentile: below the 20th is Very Poor to Poor, 20th–39th Fair, 40th–59th Average, 60th–79th Good, 80th–94th Excellent, and 95th or above Superior.[^acsm] Untrained fitness falls about 10% per decade after age 30; regular aerobic training roughly halves that decline to about 5% per decade.[^friend2015]

<Aside type="note">
Cycle-ergometer (stationary-bike) tests typically read about 10–15% lower than treadmill tests, and wearable estimates carry meaningful error. Compare like with like, and read your own trend over weeks rather than one figure against the table.
</Aside>

## How to read your activity

The weekly activity target is identical across the WHO 2020, US HHS 2018 and AHA guidelines. The 2018/2020 revisions also dropped the old rule that exercise had to come in ten-minute bouts — any duration now counts toward the total.[^who2020][^hhs2018]

| Target | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Moderate-intensity aerobic | 150–300 min/week |
| Vigorous-intensity aerobic | 75–150 min/week (1 vigorous min ≈ 2 moderate) |
| Muscle-strengthening | ≥2 days/week, all major muscle groups |
| Older adults | add multicomponent/balance training ≥3 days/week |

Benefit continues to accrue beyond 300 minutes of moderate or 150 minutes of vigorous activity per week — the upper figure is a strong floor, not a ceiling.

### Steps per day

There is no official WHO or HHS step target; the evidence comes from observational cohorts. Mortality risk falls progressively up to roughly **6,000–8,000 steps/day for adults aged 60 and over** and **8,000–10,000 for adults under 60**, after which the curve flattens.[^paluch2022] Crossing about 7,000 steps a day is associated with substantially lower mortality than staying below it,[^paluch2021] and dose-response benefit continues to around 12,000 steps a day.[^saintmaurice2020] The familiar "10,000 steps" figure is a 1960s pedometer marketing slogan, not a research finding.

### Intensity zones

Intensity is relative to your own fitness. The ACSM classifies it by percentage of maximum heart rate (%HRmax), percentage of heart-rate reserve (%HRR, the Karvonen method) or perceived effort.

| Intensity | %HRmax | %HRR | Effort / talk test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | 57–63% | 30–39% | — |
| Moderate | 64–76% | 40–59% | talk but not sing |
| Vigorous | 77–95% | 60–89% | only a few words at a time |
| Near-maximal | ≥96% | ≥90% | — |

Heart-rate reserve is HRmax − resting HR; target HR = (intensity × HRR) + resting HR. A population estimate of maximum heart rate is about 208 − (0.7 × age),[^tanaka2001] or the older 220 − age — both are crude, and a tested maximum beats either. Beta-blockers blunt heart rate and break every HR-based zone; the talk test or perceived effort is the fallback.[^acsm]

## What changes it

Aerobic fitness rises with consistent training and falls with detraining, age, illness, altitude, anaemia and several cardiac and pulmonary conditions. The training principles that move it safely are unglamorous: about 24–48 hours of recovery between sessions stressing the same muscle group, resistance work at least twice a week per muscle group, and progressive overload that changes one variable at a time in small steps (a soft, non-official rule of thumb is no more than about 10% per week).[^acsm] Rest is part of the program, not a break from it.

<Aside type="caution">
Genuine overtraining is a *cluster* that must co-occur over about a week — a persistently elevated resting heart rate, suppressed [heart-rate variability](/methodology/recovery/), poor sleep, declining performance and low mood together — and it overlaps heavily with ordinary illness, stress and life load.[^meeusen2013] A single off day means little. If fitness is falling alongside breathlessness, chest pain or unexplained fatigue, that is a clinician conversation, not a training adjustment.
</Aside>

Two related cautions on the numbers themselves: wearable active-energy ("calories burned") estimates commonly carry ±20–30% error and should be read as motivational trend, not a basis for eating decisions — particularly for anyone with disordered-eating risk.[^who2020] And hydration follows thirst and body-mass change, not a universal millilitres-per-hour figure; intakes much above ~0.8 L/h raise the risk of dilutional hyponatraemia, so more is not safer.[^acsmfluid]
