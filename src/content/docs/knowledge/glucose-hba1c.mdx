Glucose is the body's primary circulating fuel, and the amount in your blood at any moment reflects a balance struck by diet, activity, hormones and — when it is present — insulin. HealthLog tracks it three ways, because each answers a different question.

## What the numbers measure

- **Fasting plasma glucose (FPG)** — a single reading after at least 8 hours without caloric intake. It captures your baseline when no recent meal is in play.
- **Postprandial glucose** — a reading after eating, classically 2 hours into a standardised 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). It captures how quickly your system clears a carbohydrate load.
- **HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin)** — the fraction of haemoglobin that has bound glucose, integrated over the ~2–3 month lifespan of a red blood cell. It is the long-run average, not a snapshot.

A continuous glucose monitor adds a fourth view: thousands of interstitial readings a day, summarised as **time in range** rather than a single value.

<Aside type="note" title="Plasma vs fingerstick">
Diagnostic thresholds are defined on venous plasma. A capillary fingerstick meter reads slightly below venous plasma, so treat home-meter numbers as oriented to — not identical to — the lab cut-points below.
</Aside>

## Why it matters

Persistently raised glucose marks prediabetes and is central to diagnosing and managing type-2 diabetes.[^ada2025] Chronic hyperglycaemia drives both microvascular damage (eyes, kidneys, nerves) and macrovascular disease, and the window where lifestyle change still prevents progression opens long before symptoms do — which is the entire case for tracking it early.

## How to read your numbers

All thresholds below are general screening and management references, not a diagnosis. A single abnormal reading never diagnoses anything: the ADA requires a repeat abnormal test on a separate day for confirmation.[^ada2025]

### Fasting plasma glucose

| Category | Range (mg/dL · mmol/L) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Normal (ADA) | &lt;100 · &lt;5.6 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Prediabetes / IFG (ADA) | 100–125 · 5.6–6.9 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Prediabetes / IFG (WHO) | 110–125 · 6.1–6.9 | WHO/IDF, 2006[^who2006] |
| Diabetes (confirmed) | ≥126 · ≥7.0 | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025][^who2006] |

The lower bound of the prediabetes band is where the two bodies disagree — ADA opens it at 100 mg/dL, WHO at 110. The diabetes threshold (≥126 mg/dL) is shared. For non-pregnant adults already living with diabetes, the ADA preprandial **target** is 80–130 mg/dL (4.4–7.2 mmol/L).[^ada2025]

### Postprandial / 2-hour OGTT

ADA and WHO/IDF use identical 2-hour cut-points here.

| Category | Range (mg/dL · mmol/L) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | &lt;140 · &lt;7.8 | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025][^who2006] |
| Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) | 140–199 · 7.8–11.0 | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025][^who2006] |
| Diabetes | ≥200 · ≥11.1 | ADA 2025; WHO/IDF 2006[^ada2025][^who2006] |

A random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL with classic symptoms is independently diagnostic.[^ada2025] The ADA peak-postprandial **target** for adults with diabetes is &lt;180 mg/dL (&lt;10.0 mmol/L), measured 1–2 hours after a meal.

### HbA1c

| Category | Range (% · mmol/mol) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | &lt;5.7 · &lt;39 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Prediabetes | 5.7–6.4 · 39–46 | ADA, 2025[^ada2025] |
| Diabetes (confirmed) | ≥6.5 · ≥48 | ADA 2025; WHO 2011[^ada2025][^who2011] |

WHO endorses the ≥6.5% diabetes cut-off but does not define a formal prediabetes HbA1c band — a difference of scope, not a contradiction.[^who2011] The ADA management **target** for most non-pregnant adults with diabetes is &lt;7.0% (&lt;53 mmol/mol); ≤6.5% for selected low-risk individuals; and a deliberately looser &lt;8.0% (&lt;64 mmol/mol) where life expectancy is limited, comorbidity is extensive, or hypoglycaemia risk is high.[^ada2025]

### Continuous glucose monitoring — time in range

| Metric | Target (most non-pregnant adults with diabetes) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Time in Range 70–180 mg/dL (3.9–10.0 mmol/L) | >70% (~16.8 h/day) | Battelino et al., 2019[^battelino] |
| Time Below Range &lt;70 / &lt;54 mg/dL | &lt;4% / &lt;1% | Battelino et al., 2019[^battelino] |
| Time Above Range >180 / >250 mg/dL | &lt;25% / &lt;5% | Battelino et al., 2019[^battelino] |
| Coefficient of variation | ≤36% (stable glucose) | Battelino et al., 2019[^battelino] |

As orientation, a time in range near 70% corresponds roughly to an HbA1c near 7%. Older or high-risk adults work to a relaxed >50% in range with &lt;1% below 70 mg/dL; pregnancy in type-1 diabetes uses a tighter 63–140 mg/dL band with >70% in range.[^battelino] These figures assume diabetes management — they are not a non-diabetic "optimisation" target.

<Aside type="note" title="CGM caveats">
Time-in-range metrics are valid only with adequate sensor wear (at least 14 days at ≥70% active data). A device's glucose management indicator (GMI) is an estimate of, not a substitute for, a lab HbA1c.
</Aside>

### Converting between units

- Glucose: **mg/dL ÷ 18.0 = mmol/L**.
- HbA1c: **mmol/mol = (% − 2.15) × 10.929**; **% = mmol/mol × 0.09148 + 2.152**.

## What changes your numbers

Glucose responds to far more than the last meal. Acute illness, physical or emotional stress, corticosteroids and a recent carbohydrate load all push readings up, sometimes sharply and temporarily. Sleep and meal timing matter too: even one night of partial sleep deprivation measurably lowers insulin sensitivity, and glucose tolerance falls across the day so that an identical meal produces a higher spike in the evening than the morning.[^cross] A poor night or a late, large dinner is a plausible contributor to an isolated next-morning fasting anomaly — not a diagnosis of anything.

Targets themselves individualise. Generally healthy adults aim for HbA1c &lt;7%; frail older adults with limited life expectancy work to a less stringent 7.5–8%, because avoiding hypoglycaemia outranks chasing a low number.[^cross] Medication shapes the picture too: metformin monotherapy does not cause hypoglycaemia, whereas insulin and sulfonylureas can.[^cross]

<Aside type="caution" title="When HbA1c can mislead">
HbA1c is unreliable wherever red-cell turnover or haemoglobin is altered — haemolytic or iron-deficiency anaemia, recent transfusion, pregnancy, haemoglobinopathies, erythropoietin therapy, advanced kidney disease. When HbA1c and direct glucose readings disagree in these settings, the glucose reading takes precedence.[^ada2025]
</Aside>

## When to act

<Aside type="caution" title="Low glucose — act on the number">
A glucose **&lt;70 mg/dL (&lt;3.9 mmol/L)** is a hypoglycaemia alert regardless of symptoms: treat with fast-acting carbohydrate and re-check in 15 minutes. **&lt;54 mg/dL (&lt;3.0 mmol/L)** is clinically significant and needs immediate action. Any episode requiring help from another person is severe by definition — and is defined by the impairment, not by a number.[^redflags]
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="Very high glucose — do not be falsely reassured by a single number">
Sustained very high glucose (commonly ≥250–300 mg/dL) with symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, vomiting, dehydration, fast breathing or altered thinking is a reason to seek care promptly. Diabetic ketoacidosis can occur at glucose as low as 200 mg/dL, and roughly one in ten cases is euglycaemic (under 200) — so a glucose figure alone cannot rule it out. If you feel unwell, a "normal-looking" reading is not an all-clear.[^redflags]
</Aside>

The everyday signals — a fasting reading that has crept into the prediabetes band, an HbA1c trending upward — are matters for an unhurried conversation with a clinician, not an alarm. A meaningful change in serial HbA1c is roughly half a percentage point or more; smaller wobbles sit inside normal assay and biological variation.[^cross]

## How this connects to your other metrics

Glucose does not move in isolation. Weight is the most actionable lever most people have: a sustained 5–10% loss meaningfully improves glycaemia alongside blood pressure and lipids. Central fat in particular tracks with insulin resistance, which is why the [body-composition](/knowledge/body-composition/) and [weight and BMI](/knowledge/weight-bmi/) pages are worth reading alongside this one. Sleep regularity and meal timing feed back into your morning fasting number.
