Where your fat sits matters as much as how much you carry. Waist circumference and the ratios derived from it — waist-to-hip and waist-to-height — are simple tape-measure proxies for central, and specifically visceral, fat: the metabolically active fat packed around the abdominal organs. This page is the deeper dive that complements the waist sub-section on the [weight, BMI and waist measures](/knowledge/weight-bmi/) page; the two are meant to agree, with this one going further on visceral fat, the ratios and measurement technique.

## Why it matters

Not all body fat carries the same risk. Subcutaneous fat sits under the skin and is relatively benign; **visceral fat** wraps the liver, pancreas and intestines and is far more metabolically harmful, driving insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia and inflammation. Central adiposity predicts cardiometabolic risk independently of BMI — which is why a tape around the waist often tells you more than the number on the scale, and why two people at the same BMI can carry very different risk.[^who2008] Elevated waist circumference is one of the defining criteria of the metabolic syndrome.[^idf]

## How to read your numbers

These are orientation bands for screening, not diagnostic verdicts, and the thresholds below are for European-origin populations. Ancestry-specific cut-offs are lower — many guidelines use roughly ≥90 cm for men and ≥80 cm for women in South Asian, Chinese and other Asian populations, reflecting higher cardiometabolic risk at a smaller waist.[^idf]

| Measure | Increased risk | Substantially increased | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waist circumference, men (European-origin) | >94 cm | >102 cm | WHO 2008; NIH/NHLBI 1998 [^who2008][^nih] |
| Waist circumference, women (European-origin) | >80 cm | >88 cm | WHO 2008; NIH/NHLBI 1998 [^who2008][^nih] |
| Waist circumference, Asian populations | men ≥90 cm / women ≥80 cm | — | IDF metabolic-syndrome criteria [^idf] |
| Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) | men >0.90 / women >0.85 | — | WHO, 2008 [^who2008] |
| Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) | ≥0.5 (keep your waist under half your height) | — | NICE; Ashwell [^nice][^ashwell] |

<Aside type="note" title="Waist-to-height ratio is the most portable rule">
The waist-to-height ratio needs no population table: keep your waist circumference under half your height, and the boundary holds across sexes and ancestries.[^nice][^ashwell] Because it scales the waist to body size, it sidesteps the separate male/female and ancestry-specific waist cut-offs and is an easy figure to track over time. NICE recommends it alongside BMI for people with a BMI under 35.[^nice]
</Aside>

<Aside type="note" title="Measure the same way every time">
Waist circumference is technique-sensitive — a casual measurement varies by centimetres. Use the midpoint between the lowest rib and the top of the hip bone (iliac crest), with the tape horizontal and snug but not compressing the skin, taken at the end of a normal exhale. Standardise the time of day, breathing phase and tape tension between readings so the trend reflects your body, not your technique.[^who2008]
</Aside>

## What changes it

Central fat responds to the same levers as overall adiposity — energy balance, physical activity, sleep and alcohol — and visceral fat is often among the first depots to shrink with a modest, sustained weight loss, so the waist can improve before the scale moves much. Day to day, the *measurement* wobbles with bloating, meal timing, posture and tape placement far more than the underlying fat does, which is why consistent technique and a multi-week view matter. Body-fat distribution also shifts with age and sex: men tend toward central fat earlier, and the loss of oestrogen around menopause moves fat storage toward the abdomen in women. Ancestry shifts the risk a given waist represents, as the table notes.

## When to act

<Aside type="caution" title="A high or rising waist is a prompt, not a diagnosis">
A waist circumference in the substantially-increased band (over ~102 cm for men, ~88 cm for women, or the lower ancestry-specific cut-offs), or a waist-to-height ratio at or above 0.5, is worth discussing with a clinician — especially alongside raised [blood glucose](/knowledge/glucose-hba1c/), blood pressure or lipids, which together point toward metabolic syndrome.[^idf] A rapidly enlarging abdomen that is not explained by weight change, or abdominal swelling with pain, is a different matter and needs prompt medical assessment. General guidance, not medical advice; individual variation is wide.
</Aside>

## How this connects

Waist measures are the distribution half of the question the scale cannot answer — read this page with [weight, BMI and waist measures](/knowledge/weight-bmi/), which sets the waist figures in the context of overall body weight. Because the tape cannot tell fat from muscle, it pairs with [body composition](/knowledge/body-composition/) for a fuller account of what you carry and where. And because visceral fat and glucose handling travel together, central adiposity is one of the strongest day-to-day companions of [blood glucose and HbA1c](/knowledge/glucose-hba1c/).
