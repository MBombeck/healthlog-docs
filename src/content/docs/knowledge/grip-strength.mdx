Grip strength is the maximal force your hand produces when you squeeze a hand-held dynamometer, recorded in kilograms or pounds. It is one of the cheapest, most repeatable measurements in all of physiology, and it punches far above its weight: a single squeeze correlates with whole-body strength and tracks long-term health better than almost any other field test of muscle.

## Why it matters

Grip is a proxy. The force in your hand reflects the strength of muscle throughout your body, and muscle strength in turn reflects nutrition, activity, neuromuscular health and biological ageing. In a large international cohort, each 5 kg lower grip strength was associated with a higher risk of death from any cause and from cardiovascular disease — a stronger predictor of mortality than systolic blood pressure in that analysis.[^pure] It is also a core diagnostic criterion for **sarcopenia**, the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength that erodes independence and raises fall and fracture risk.[^ewgsop2] Because it is quick, painless and reliable, grip is an ideal thing to measure a few times a year and read as a trend.

## How to read your numbers

These are orientation bands, not targets. Grip falls naturally with age and differs by sex and hand size, so the most useful comparison is against your own earlier readings and against the diagnostic floors used to screen for low strength. Use your dominant hand, sit with the elbow at 90°, and take the best of three squeezes.

| Group | Typical / orientation grip | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Men, young-adult peak (~30–39 y) | roughly 45–50 kg dominant hand | Bohannon normative data [^bohannon] |
| Women, young-adult peak (~30–39 y) | roughly 27–31 kg dominant hand | Bohannon normative data [^bohannon] |
| Men, ~60–69 y | roughly 35–40 kg | Bohannon normative data [^bohannon] |
| Women, ~60–69 y | roughly 22–25 kg | Bohannon normative data [^bohannon] |
| Sarcopenia screening floor, men | &lt;27 kg | EWGSOP2, 2019 [^ewgsop2] |
| Sarcopenia screening floor, women | &lt;16 kg | EWGSOP2, 2019 [^ewgsop2] |

<Aside type="note" title="The trend in your own grip is the signal">
Absolute norms vary with hand size, instrument and protocol, so a one-off number against a population table is a rough orientation at best. A *falling* grip measured the same way over months or years is the more informative reading — it can flag deconditioning or early sarcopenia before it shows up anywhere else. Standardise the dynamometer, posture and hand each time so the comparison is fair.
</Aside>

## What changes it

Grip is technique- and equipment-sensitive: dynamometer model, calibration, hand chosen, elbow and wrist position, encouragement and time of day all move a reading by several kilograms, which is why a consistent protocol matters more than the device. Acute factors — fatigue, a recent heavy workout, pain or stiffness in the hand or wrist, dehydration, illness — temporarily lower it. Over the long run, grip rises with resistance training and falls with inactivity, undernutrition (particularly low protein intake), and the steady decline of ageing, which accelerates after roughly the sixth decade. Arthritis or any hand, wrist or nerve injury makes grip an unreliable proxy for whole-body strength in that person.

The constructive response to a low or declining grip is rarely to train the hand in isolation. Progressive resistance training of the major muscle groups, two or more sessions a week, is the evidence-based way to build and preserve strength across the body — and the grip number tends to follow.[^acsm]

## When to act

<Aside type="caution" title="Low or falling grip with other signs">
A grip below the sarcopenia screening floor (under ~27 kg for men, ~16 kg for women), or a clear unexplained decline over time — especially alongside unintentional [weight](/knowledge/weight-bmi/) loss, slowing walking speed, more frequent falls, or new fatigue — is worth raising with a clinician, who can assess for sarcopenia and its causes.[^ewgsop2] A sudden loss of grip in one hand, or grip weakness with numbness, drooping or trouble speaking, is different — sudden one-sided weakness can signal a stroke and is a medical emergency: call emergency services. General guidance, not medical advice; individual variation is wide.
</Aside>

## How this connects

Grip strength and cardiorespiratory fitness are the two field-measurable pillars of physical capacity, and they predict longevity through partly different routes — read grip alongside [VO₂max and activity](/knowledge/vo2max-activity/) for the fuller picture. Because grip is a strength proxy and muscle is metabolically active tissue, it also complements [body composition](/knowledge/body-composition/): muscle mass and the strength it produces tell related but distinct halves of the story, and an unchanging body weight can hide a quiet shift from muscle toward fat.
