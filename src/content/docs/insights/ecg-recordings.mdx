When a compatible watch records a single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) and that
recording syncs in, HealthLog stores it and shows it on the **Insights** page as
its own surface — next to the rhythm and vitals surfaces the page already carries.
It is a **display** of a recording your device made, not a reading HealthLog
performs on the trace.

The ECG surface rides the **Insights** module. Enable it under **Settings →
Modules**; with the module off, the surface does not appear even when recordings
have synced.

## What the surface shows

Each recording renders on the **calibration grid** cardiologists read on paper —
the familiar 25 mm/s horizontal, 10 mm/mV vertical layout, so the trace has the
right shape and proportions at a glance rather than an arbitrary auto-scaled
line. Around the trace, each recording carries:

- **Date and time** it was taken.
- **Duration** of the recording (a single-lead strip is typically 30 seconds).
- **Average heart rate** over the recording.
- **The device's own result** — see below.

Recordings list newest first, so the most recent strip is the one you open.

## The result belongs to the device, not to HealthLog

The classification shown with a recording is the **recording device's own
result**, attributed to the device. HealthLog collapses the device-native
labels into three verdicts on import — "Atrial fibrillation detected", "No
signs of atrial fibrillation", and "Inconclusive recording" — and does not
re-run, second-guess, or add to that classification.

**HealthLog does not read or interpret the waveform.** There is no model, no
heuristic, and no analysis of the trace anywhere in the app. The strip is drawn
so you can see it and hand it to a clinician; the words beside it are the ones
your device produced, labelled as such.

:::caution[This is not a diagnosis]
A permanent note sits with every recording: a single-lead ECG and its device
classification are **not a diagnosis**. A "normal" result does not rule a
condition out, and a device is not a cardiologist. This is the
[non-diagnostic standard](/explanation/non-diagnostic-standard/) applied to the
one surface where it matters most.
:::

## The attribution line

Alongside the strip, the device's result carries an explicit attribution:
"Recorded result: …, as reported by the recording device." The average heart
rate and duration render as their own metadata rows beside it. Everything on
the surface restates what your device recorded; nothing adds a reading of its
own. A fresh recording also surfaces gently in the
[Today view](/insights/today/).

## When a result is not normal

When the device's own classification is anything other than a normal one, the
surface points you to a clinician — plainly, without alarm. A single non-normal
strip is common and often benign; a recurring pattern is worth showing to someone
who can examine you and confirm it with a clinical ECG. HealthLog's role stops at
showing you the recording and making it easy to share: it never tells you what
the result means for your health.

For the wider context on why an irregular reading is a screening prompt rather
than a verdict, see
[resting heart rate — irregular-rhythm and atrial-fibrillation awareness](/knowledge/resting-heart-rate/#irregular-rhythm-and-atrial-fibrillation-awareness).

## What the AI never sees

The waveform trace is a picture your device recorded, not a health metric the
Coach or insights reason over. No provider is ever sent the trace or asked to
interpret it. What the insight-generation snapshot may carry is a small
device-verdict summary: how many recordings exist, the device's own verdicts,
and the latest recording's recency and average heart rate. The prompt binds
that block to a strict device-verdict-only rule — the model may note that a
recording exists and attribute its result to the device, and must never
present a verdict as its own or read the waveform.

## Sharing it

An ECG recording is viewable in the app and is covered by the full backup
export. It does not print in the doctor-report PDF or the health-record
export, so for an appointment, show the strip on the device or export the
backup. A clinician reading it in context is the reading that counts.
