Two accounts on the same instance can be connected so that one of them can open
the other's health record. A parent and an adult child, two partners, somebody
keeping an eye on a relative's blood pressure: the case this exists for is a
household.

Both people keep their own account, their own password and their own second
factor. Nobody signs in as anybody else. What you are handing over is a grant
from your account to theirs, and that grant carries two things you choose when
you send the invitation: a level, which decides what they may do, and a scope,
which decides how much of the record they can open.

If the person you want to look after has no account at all, and never will
because they are a child or somebody in your care, the thing you want is a
[managed profile](/guides/managed-profiles/) rather than a grant.

:::note[Three levels, and none of them is full access]
A grant is **read**, **write** or **manage**. You choose when you send the
invitation, and the choice is fixed for the life of that grant.

Read lets somebody open your record and change nothing in it. Write lets them
add entries from a short, closed list. Manage lets them change and remove what
is already recorded, including entries you made yourself, and read the insights
generated from the record.

What none of the three reaches is the account around the record: your login,
your second factor, your connected services, your API tokens, where your
notifications go, which modules and thresholds are on, and who else has access.
That line is drawn on the server, and it does not move with the level.

There is no way to raise a grant you already sent. Moving somebody up a level
means ending the grant they have and inviting them again at the level you mean,
which they accept again. The higher levels need both consents, yours because it
is your record and theirs because they are being asked to write into somebody
else's health record. Acceptance is the one moment those two meet, so it happens
once per grant rather than being edited afterwards by one side.
:::

## Before you start

The person you want to invite needs an account on this instance already. There
is no invite-by-e-mail flow here; if they have never registered, they have to do
that first, either through open registration or through an
[invite link](/guides/invites/) from your operator.

You will invite them by the username or e-mail address they type into the login
form. If you are the operator too, that is the name you see in the admin user
list.

Offering **manage** can only be done in a browser, and if your account has a
second factor enrolled, it asks for a fresh one at the moment you send the
invitation. The other two levels can
be offered from anywhere, and accepting any of the three can be done anywhere.

## Send the invitation

1. Open **Settings → Shared access**.
2. Under **Invite someone**, enter the username or e-mail address they sign in
   with.
3. Under **What they may do**, pick the level. **Can view** is the default.
   **Can view and add** is the write level and **Can manage** is the third; the
   form spells out underneath each one exactly what it covers.
4. Under **What they can open**, either leave **Entire record** selected or
   choose **Only the sections you pick** and tick the sections. Manage access
   always covers the entire record, so the choice disappears when you select it.
5. Optionally fill in **Access ends on**. Leave it empty and the grant runs
   until somebody ends it.
6. Choose **Send invitation**.

Nothing is shared yet. The invitation sits in their own settings and gives them
nothing until they accept it, and until then your record is exactly as private
as it was a minute ago. The level and the scope travel with the invitation, so
what they accept is what you offered.

If the name you typed does not belong to an account on this instance, the form
says so rather than creating a pending invitation nobody will ever see. That is
deliberate: a silent pending row after a typo is a worse failure than a clear
refusal.

The endpoint allows ten invitations an hour per account. If you are setting up
several people at once and hit that, wait and carry on.

## Accepting an invitation

For the person who was invited:

1. Open **Settings → Shared access**.
2. The invitation appears under **Records I can open**.
3. Choose **Accept**.

At the write and manage levels the screen states what accepting means before
you do it: entries you add become part of somebody else's record, and at the
manage level everything you do is recorded under your own name in their activity
view. Acceptance is the moment access begins, and the grant row keeps the
timestamp.

## Opening somebody's record

Once an invitation is accepted, the record appears in the user menu under **Open
a record**. Choosing it reloads the app into that person's record.

A strip across the top of every page names whose record is open and what the
grant admits, with a button back to your own. It is loud on purpose. Somebody
who forgets they are switched will read the wrong week, or log their own weight
into another person's record, and an entry that was never theirs is a mess to
unpick.

To leave, use the button in the strip or pick **My own record** from the same
menu.

If you have the app open in more than one browser tab, switching records moves
the whole session rather than the one tab. The other tabs notice, re-read who
they are and land in the same record as the tab you switched in. See
[Two tabs, one record](#two-tabs-one-record) below.

## What each level may do

**Read** opens the record and changes nothing in it.

**Write** adds these, and this list is the whole of it:

- A measurement, single or in a batch. Weight, a blood-pressure reading, a
  glucose value, anything the record tracks as a reading.
- A lab result. If the result names an analyte your catalogue has never seen,
  that analyte is added to your catalogue with it.
- A biomarker in your catalogue, on its own.
- An illness episode.
- A side effect recorded against one of your medications.
- A medication, with its schedule.
- Marking a dose taken or skipped, one at a time or by clearing everything due
  at once.

Every member of that list makes something new. A write-level delegate cannot
correct the medication they added ten seconds ago with a typo in the dose, and
cannot delete a reading they entered. Editing, deleting, restoring a deleted
entry, bulk actions and importing a file are yours.

**Manage** adds the other half. A manager can change and remove entries,
including ones you wrote yourself, can record your health background (allergies,
family history and the facts under Anamnesis), and can read the insights
generated from the record. Everything they do is recorded under their own name,
with a verb that says what it was rather than "made a change".

Two refusals hold at every level, manage included.

**Deferring one of your reminders is yours alone.** Marking a dose and pushing a
reminder back an hour look like the same action from the medication card, and
they are not. Recording that a dose went down is a fact about your day.
Postponing the reminder you set for yourself is a change to your schedule, made
by somebody who does not have to live with it.

**A dose that already has a decision keeps it.** Re-sending the same decision is
fine, because a second tap, a phone catching up after no signal and a retry
after a partial sweep all look like that. Turning your "taken" into a "skipped"
is not, at any level.

The document vault is read at every level. A delegate can open the list, read a
document and open its file, because a clinical letter is health data belonging to
the record and reading it is much of the reason to hand somebody the record.
Uploading, retyping, discarding, linking a document to an illness episode and
every AI action stay with you.

Saving confirms where the entry went, naming the record by its owner, so a
mistake is visible at the moment it happens rather than a week later.

## Sharing only part of the record

An invitation can name the sections it opens. Everything you do not pick stays
closed, and a section you kept back is refused exactly the way a record nobody
shared with you is, so what you held back is not visible from the outside.

There are eight sections.

| Section | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Readings | Weight, blood pressure, pulse, glucose, sleep, workouts and the values you track. |
| Medications | The medication list, its schedules, the doses taken, side effects and stock. |
| Lab results | Lab results and the list of analytes behind them. |
| Health background | Allergies, family history, the facts in your health profile, doctor visits and the practitioner address book, and the vaccination log. |
| Illness | Illness episodes and the day logs kept under them. |
| Mood and mind | Mood entries and the history of the mental-health questionnaires. |
| Cycle | Cycle tracking. |
| Documents | Everything stored in the document vault. |

Two things the invitation screen says out loud rather than leaving you to work
them out.

**A section carries whatever was written in it.** Notes and names inside one
section can mention the rest of your health, so sharing a section shares
everything written in it. The vault is the sharpest case, because a document can
contain anything at all; share that section only if the person may read all of
it.

**The whole-record views need the whole record.** The dashboard overview, the
health score and the daily digest appear only when the entire record is shared,
because a figure computed from part of a record would read as a figure about the
person.

Three more properties worth knowing.

- **Manage is always whole-record.** There is no way to hand somebody everything
  about part of you, for the reason above: a note in one section can be about any
  other. An invitation that names both is refused rather than quietly narrowed.
- **A scope never grows.** A grant written when this list held eight sections
  opens those of the eight it named and nothing added in a later release. A new
  section is a consent question, so it takes a new invitation.
- **Every grant written before v1.37.0 opens the entire record** and is
  untouched. Nobody has to agree to anything again.

## What a delegate cannot reach at any level

**Nothing about the account around the record.** Not the password, passkeys,
second factor or recovery codes. Not the sessions or trusted devices. Not API
tokens or the MCP connector. Not the connected services, so they cannot re-point
a sync or feed their own wearable into your record. Not the notification
channels, so your reminders cannot be redirected to another phone. Not the
exports, the health-record archive or the clinician share links. Not the Coach,
and not generating a fresh insight, because that sends your data to a provider
under a consent you gave for your own use. Not sharing itself, so a delegate can
neither invite a third person nor pass their access on.

Display preferences stay with the person at the keyboard. Reading somebody
else's record does not change your own theme, language or dashboard layout.

These are refusals the server makes rather than screens that happen to be
hidden. What the screens do is match them: a control the record will not accept
is simply not drawn, so a delegate never fills in a form that was going to be
turned down. The reasoning is written up in
[What a shared record is](/explanation/record-sharing/).

## Whose entry is it

An entry a delegate makes is stored as yours. It carries your account, appears
in your charts and exports, and reads the same as anything you typed in
yourself. It is a fact about your body, and it belongs in your record whoever
happened to be holding the phone.

Who wrote it down is kept in the audit trail rather than on the row. That is a
deliberate split. The record stays one person's, so nothing in it has to be
read twice to work out whether it counts, and the question "who put this here"
still has an answer months later.

## See what the access is being used for

**Settings → Shared access** answers three questions for the owner at any time.

- **Who can open my record** lists everybody with access, at which level and
  over which sections, when they accepted, and when they last opened it. Access
  that has ended stays on the list, so you can always see who had it and between
  which dates.
- **When my record was opened** is a day-by-day view: one line per person per
  day, with how many times.
- The same view names what was done, not only that somebody was there. A line
  reads "added a reading", "marked a dose", "changed a lab result", "removed an
  illness entry", and a refused attempt is on the list too. Nothing a delegate
  does is silent, and nothing they do is disguised as your own doing.

Two things the list states about itself, and both are worth reading.

**How far back it reaches.** The view is bounded by your instance's audit
retention, which defaults to 365 days and is set with
`AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS`. The page prints the number your instance actually
uses rather than a hardcoded one, and so does the dialog that ends somebody's
access. An empty list means nobody opened the record inside that window, not
that nobody ever did.

**Whether you are seeing all of it.** A busy record can produce more activity
than one read returns. When that happens the page says it is showing the most
recent activity rather than all of it, so the oldest line you can see never
reads as the beginning.

Marking a dose also reaches you at the time it happens. When somebody records
one of your doses as taken or skipped, you are notified on whatever channels you
already have switched on, naming the person and the medication. It is on by
default and turns off like any other notification under **Settings →
Notifications**. A dose you mark yourself never notifies you.

## Two tabs, one record

Switching records moves the browser session, not the tab you clicked in. That is
the right behaviour for the common case and it used to leave a stale tab behind:
a second tab, left open on the record you had just left, went on reading and
writing there until somebody reloaded it.

Every request a browser makes now carries the record that tab believes it is in.
A request naming a record the session has left is refused, and the tab recovers
by re-reading who it is and re-rendering, which takes no interaction from you.
The refusal is a reconciliation signal rather than an error, and nothing about it
is shown as a failure.

A session that has never opened somebody else's record is unaffected, and so is
the phone app, which names the record on each request and never had a selector
to go stale. Operators reading logs will find the same behaviour described from
their side under
[Sharing shows a 409 in the logs](/self-hosting/troubleshooting/#sharing-shows-a-409-in-the-logs).

## End the access

Either side can end it, and neither needs a second factor or a typed
confirmation. Reducing access should never be harder than granting it was.

- **As the owner:** **Settings → Shared access → Who can open my record**,
  then **End access**. If the invitation was never accepted, the same row offers
  **Withdraw**.
- **As the delegate:** **Settings → Shared access → Records I can open**, then
  **Hand back**.

It takes effect on their very next request, not at their next login. The grant is
checked against the database on every request, so there is no cached verdict to
wait out. If their browser is sitting inside your record when you end it, that
same browser lands back in their own account.

What ending access cannot do is take back what was already read. Somebody who
had access could have remembered it, screenshotted it, or written it down. The
product can close the door and cannot reach through it, and the confirmation
dialog says so.

Ending access never deletes the record of it. The row stays with its dates and
with who ended it. Inviting the same person again creates a new row beside the
old one, and they have to accept again.

The one refusal here is on a managed profile, where the last guardian may not
leave the record with nobody looking after it. That is covered in
[Records with no login of their own](/guides/managed-profiles/#the-last-guardian).

## Access that lapses on a date

A grant can carry a date it lapses on, set in the **Access ends on** field when
you send the invitation. The date is checked against the clock on every request
rather than by a sweep that may not have run, so a grant one second past it is
already inert.

The field is optional and empty by default, because the common household case is
access that simply runs. It earns its place when the access has a known end: a
stay in hospital, a course of treatment, a relative visiting for a month. The
earliest date you can pick is tomorrow, and the access runs to the end of the
day you name.

The date is fixed when the invitation is sent. There is no screen for extending
or shortening it afterwards, so a different date means ending the grant and
inviting again. Through the API it is `expiresAt` on
`POST /api/account/grants`. See
[Shared access endpoints](/reference/api-endpoints/#shared-access).

## Sharing with a clinician is a different thing

**Settings → Shared access** is for another account on this instance.
**Settings → Sharing** mints a public, time-boxed, passphrase-protected link for
somebody with no account at all, which is what you hand a doctor at the desk.
The two do not overlap, and a delegate cannot mint one of those links from
inside your record. See
[Sharing documents with a clinician](/guides/document-vault/#sharing-documents-with-a-clinician).

## For operators

Nothing to configure. Sharing is available on every instance, needs no
environment variable to switch on, and does nothing until one account invites
another.

Four things are worth knowing before somebody asks.

- An invitation to a username nobody uses is refused by name, which tells the
  caller that the name is free. The caller is already authenticated and is
  limited to ten invitations an hour, which bounds how fast the surface could be
  walked.
- Deleting an account removes the grants it is party to. Audit rows naming that
  account as the one who opened somebody's record stay and render as "a deleted
  account", because nulling them would turn "your daughter opened your record"
  into "you opened your record".
- Write and manage access raise what your audit retention is worth. Authorship
  of a delegate's entry lives in the audit trail and nowhere else, so
  `AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS` is the window in which "who entered this" has an
  answer. The entry itself is permanent; the name attached to it is not.
  Shortening the retention on an instance where households share records is a
  decision, not a tidy-up. See
  [Audit-trail retention](/reference/environment-variables/#audit-trail-retention).
- A refusal a delegate earns carries no reason on the wire. Every case answers
  with the same status and the same body: a record nobody shared, a section the
  grant does not open, a write on a read grant. That is what stops the surface
  becoming a way to find out which accounts exist on your instance. The reason
  reaches the record owner's activity feed and your audit trail instead.

Backups do not carry grants. Restoring onto the same instance leaves shared
access exactly as it was; restoring onto a fresh instance means both people have
to invite and accept again. That is covered in
[Backups](/guides/backups/#what-a-backup-does-not-carry).
