Running HealthLog for yourself is a private matter and the regulation mostly
leaves it alone. Running it for other people makes you a controller of special
category data, and that is a different set of duties. This page maps the duties
onto the surfaces the app actually has, so you can see which ones are already
answered and which ones are yours to organise.

It describes software, not your situation. Nothing here is legal advice, and a
family instance and a club instance are not the same problem.

## When the regulation applies to you

The GDPR does not cover processing carried out "in the course of a purely
personal or household activity" ([Art. 2(2)(c)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-2-gdpr/)).
An instance you run for yourself alone sits there. So, on the ordinary reading,
does one you run for the people you live with, as long as nothing leaves the
household.

The exemption stops being comfortable in three cases. If you host for somebody
outside your household, a friend, a relative in another home, a club, or anyone
you have a professional relationship with, you are processing their personal
data and the exemption is gone. If you share a record outward, to a clinician
for example, that is a disclosure to a third party. And if you offer the
instance to a wider circle at all, however informally, it stops being household
activity. In all three you are the **controller**: the person who decides why
and how the data is processed.

## Who else is involved

There is no vendor behind HealthLog to sign a data-processing agreement with,
because there is no service. You run the software; nobody else holds the data by
virtue of you running it. What that leaves is a short list of parties you may
have brought in yourself.

Your hosting provider is a processor if the instance runs on rented
infrastructure. They can snapshot the disk, so [Art. 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/)
applies and you want their standard agreement on file. On your own hardware at
home the question does not arise.

An AI provider becomes a recipient of whatever the Coach and the insight
surfaces send it, which is a structured snapshot of recent health data, for as
long as their retention policy says. The key is yours, so the choice and the
contractual relationship are yours too. Pointing the app at a local endpoint
(Ollama, LM Studio, vLLM) removes the recipient entirely, and that is the option
to reach for when you are processing somebody else's health data.
[Configure AI providers](/guides/ai-providers/) covers the choice.

Notification channels are recipients of the notification text. Apple sees APNs
payloads, Telegram sees Telegram messages, and your ntfy server sees whatever
you send it. HealthLog can collapse cycle and medication reminders to a generic
title so the content is not the thing that travels, which is worth turning on
when the instance is not only yours.

## Legal basis

Health data is a special category under
[Art. 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/), and processing it is prohibited
unless one of the listed exceptions applies. For an instance you run for other
people the practical one is **explicit consent**, Art. 9(2)(a), sitting on top
of a lawful basis under [Art. 6](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/), which in the
same setting is normally consent again, Art. 6(1)(a).

Consent has to be informed, specific, freely given, and as easy to withdraw as
it was to give. In practice that means telling the person, before they start
entering anything:

- what the instance stores, which is every category they choose to track
- where it is hosted, and therefore who can seize or snapshot the machine
- that you as the administrator can read the database directly, which no
  in-app control changes
- whether an AI provider is configured, and which one
- how they get their data out and how they delete it, which are the two
  sections below
- that they can withdraw and have the record erased at any time

Write down when they agreed and what you told them. For a child's record, or
one of the [records with no login of their own](/guides/managed-profiles/), the
consent is the guardian's, and the age at which a child can consent for
themselves is set nationally between 13 and 16
([Art. 8](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-8-gdpr/)).

## Access and portability

[Art. 15](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-15-gdpr/) gives a person a copy of their data
and [Art. 20](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-20-gdpr/) gives them a machine-readable,
portable version of what they provided. Both are answered by the export surface
at **Settings, then Export**, which runs under the person's own session. You do
not have to assemble anything on their behalf; they hold the account, so they
run it themselves.

| Artefact | Format | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full backup | JSON | The single-file, user-scoped dump. The closest thing to the Art. 15 copy, and the one to hand over. |
| Per-type export | CSV or JSON | Measurements, medications, intake events, mood, chosen individually. Convenient for a spreadsheet, not a complete record. |
| Health record | PDF, FHIR R4 bundle, or a ZIP of both | The doctor-handover artefact, built from a selection you make. Written for a clinical reader, not for completeness. |

One honest limit on the full backup. Document originals and the sample-level
time series behind a workout, including GPS, are never included; the payload's
`manifest` field discloses that and the export screen says so too. Those two
workout sample-stream models are also the only entries the backup plan still
marks as pending a reader; everything else the plan marks carried is read back
by the export path. If a request has to be complete to those rows too, a
`pg_dump` filtered to the person's `userId` is the fallback.

Rectification under [Art. 16](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-16-gdpr/) needs no
special surface. Every entry in the app is editable and deletable by the person
who owns it.

## The deletion concept

[Art. 17](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/) is where the detail matters,
because "delete" means three different scopes in this app and only two of them
are erasure.

| Path | Where | Scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delete all data | Settings, then Advanced | Every row the account owns, across every user-scoped table (currently around a hundred), keeping only what the account needs to sign in again |
| Delete account | Settings, then Account | The account itself and everything the schema cascades from it |
| Wipe all data | Admin, then Danger zone | Instance-wide: every account's health data, derived from the same wipe plan since v1.37.1. See the note below. |

**Delete all data** runs as one transaction. The list of tables is declared in
one place in the source and held against the Prisma schema by a completeness
test, so a model added to the schema has to be classified as wiped, exempt, or
instance-scoped before it can ship. That test is the reason the list tracks the
schema instead of freezing at the nine tables it once was. What survives is the sign-in layer and nothing else:
passkeys, second-factor credentials and recovery codes, the session performing
the wipe, per-device refresh tokens, and the in-flight challenges that expire on
their own. Soft-deleted rows go too, because the delete carries no `deletedAt`
filter. For an account with a second factor enrolled, a fresh step-up is
required first. A single audit row recording the erasure is written after the
transaction commits, so it survives the deletion of the person's own audit
history.

**Delete account** refuses to remove the last administrator, destroys every
session, then explicitly purges the two tables that would otherwise survive with
a null owner but keep the person's IP addresses, login city, and free-text bug
reports. Then it deletes the account row and the schema's cascades take
everything attached to it, including sharing grants in both directions. A record
that would be left with no guardian at all is refused rather than orphaned.

**The admin wipe clears health data, not identity.** Since v1.37.1 it derives
from the same wipe plan as the per-account path and runs in one transaction:
every wipe-plan table is cleared, and every survivor is either a sign-in
credential, instance configuration, or the single deliberate exemption of
shared-access grants. A
build-time rule fails if the route ever names a table inline again. On builds
before v1.37.1 the route carried its own inline list, a snapshot of the schema
from the day it was written, and cleared nine tables of the 122 in the schema
while reporting the same success line however much was cleared; labs,
documents, cycle and journal entries, mood, workouts, sleep and Coach
conversations all survived that wipe.

It is still not the way to answer a request for erasure: it keeps every
account, and it wipes every account at once, which is the wrong scope for one
person invoking Art. 17. Use the per-account paths above.

Backups outlive all three paths. In-database backup rows cascade away with the
account, but objects already written to an off-host bucket stay until the
lifecycle policy expires them, and purging those is an operator action by
design: the backup code never deletes backup objects, so retention is a
bucket-lifecycle rule, and if you want that guarantee as a hard capability
bound, issue the worker credentials without delete permission. When somebody
invokes Art. 17, purge the bucket objects yourself and note in your records
that you did.

[Data deletion](/account/data-deletion/) enumerates each path model by model.

## Storage limitation

[Art. 5(1)(e)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/) says personal data is kept no
longer than necessary. Health entries are kept until the person deletes them,
which is the point of the app. The operational trails around them expire on
their own:

| Data | Default window | Variable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audit log, including login IP addresses and resolved city | 365 days | `AUDIT_LOG_RETENTION_DAYS`, floor of 7 |
| Coach conversation | 365 days | `COACH_MESSAGE_RETENTION_DAYS`, floor of 30 |
| Notification delivery attempts | 90 days | fixed |
| Host metrics | 7 days | `HOST_METRIC_RETENTION_DAYS` |
| Off-host backup objects | as configured | `BACKUP_RETENTION_DAYS` |

Shorten the audit window if you would rather hold fewer login IP addresses.
Lengthening it means holding more, so pick the number you can justify rather
than the largest one the field accepts. The account-sharing surface sends the
configured window along with the rows it shows, so a client never implies a
completeness the retention policy has already removed.

## Security of processing

[Art. 32](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-32-gdpr/) asks for measures appropriate to
the risk, and health data is high risk. What the software brings is described in
[Security and privacy](/security/overview/): AES-256-GCM at rest under a
versioned key map, passkey-first sign-in with Argon2id passwords behind it,
server-side sessions, HMAC-hashed API tokens, rate limits in the database rather
than in memory, and a strict Content-Security-Policy.

What it cannot bring is the deployment. Serve the instance over TLS, keep
`ENCRYPTION_KEYS` somewhere other than the disk holding the database, restrict
who can reach the host, keep the image current, and verify what you deployed.
[TLS certificates](/self-hosting/tls-certificates/),
[Backups](/guides/backups/), and
[Updates and deploy](/guides/updates-and-deploy/) each cover a piece of it.

## Paperwork that is yours, not the app's

The small-organisation exemption from keeping records of processing
([Art. 30(5)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-30-gdpr/)) does not extend to special
categories, so an instance holding somebody else's health data needs a record
even when it holds one person's. Keep it short and keep it current: the
categories of data, the people whose data it is, why you hold it, who else sees
it (the hosting provider, an AI provider if you configured one, the notification
services you enabled), where it is stored, the retention windows above, and the
security measures.

A breach is reportable to your supervisory authority within 72 hours of you
becoming aware of it ([Art. 33](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-33-gdpr/)), and to the
people affected without undue delay when the risk to them is high
([Art. 34](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-34-gdpr/)). A lost or copied disk holding
health data is the high-risk case, and encryption at rest only helps to the
extent the key was somewhere else. Decide now who you would notify and how, and
write down the answer, because 72 hours is not long enough to work it out from
scratch.

Every instance also serves a public privacy policy at `/privacy`, reachable
without an account. It is generic to the software and enumerates the
data-subject rights alongside the in-app surface that answers each one. It is a
useful starting text and it is not your record of processing, your consent
documentation, or a statement about your hosting.

## What the app does not do for you

There is no consent-management screen, no agreement generator, no request
tracker, and nothing that assembles an Art. 15 packet on somebody else's behalf.
Those are yours. What the app gives you is the substance the answers are made
of: an export the person runs themselves, an erasure path held to the schema by
a test, retention windows you can set, and encryption you can verify.
